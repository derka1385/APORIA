## Argument — Coherence-based One-Boxing Argument
From: The philosopher’s paradox: How to make a coherent decision in the Newcomb Problem (`oa:W2995194683`)

- **W2995194683.c001**: A rational person's intentional states are psychologically coherent across time, and rational decisions are made against this coherent backdrop.
- **W2995194683.c002**: Decisions are extended processes like a golf swing; their coherence with other psychological states links the choice to the predictor's prediction.
- **W2995194683.c004**: Psychological coherence requires certain backtracking counterfactuals to be true, which entail that choosing one box is the rational decision.
- **W2995194683.c009**: When the predictor's success is nomic, it provides counterfactual support for the prediction.
- **W2995194683.c011**: The predictor's high success rate evidences a causal process that leads to the player's decision and can be foreseen by the predictor.
- **Therefore W2995194683.c012**: Once rationality is understood in terms of psychological coherence, the rational decision in Newcomb's Problem is to choose one box.

## Challenged premise
**W2995194683.arg1.r1**: A rational agent's later intentional states need only be coherent with her earlier ones in the sense that any conflict is explained by evidence acquired since the earlier state was formed, so that apparent cross-time conflicts are defeasible revisions rather than incoherence.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · anthropic:sonnet*

Dana forms an intention on Monday to finish her thesis by June, and plans her spring around it. In March she receives evidence that her advisor will leave, making the thesis impossible to finish in time. She now intends to abandon it. Her later state conflicts with her earlier one, and the revised premise classifies this as a defeasible revision, since the conflict is explained by new evidence. But suppose Dana never learns the advisor's departure at all. Instead, in March she simply finds the thesis unappealing and drops the goal, with no new information bearing on whether June is achievable. Her earlier intention and her later state still conflict, yet nothing acquired since Monday explains the change. The revised premise says apparent cross-time conflicts are revisions only when evidence accounts for them, so it classifies Dana's shift as incoherence, which the premise itself rules out as rational-coherent. The premise therefore cannot state the coherence condition it needs: it either counts Dana's unexplained drift as coherent, which it was not meant to, or it denies that rational agents ever change course without evidence, which the argument does not license.

## Open questions

- What independent criterion distinguishes rational non-evidential revision of intention from the unexplained drift that the coherence condition was intended to rule out?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the psychological-coherence defense of one-boxing in Newcomb's Problem stands or falls on whether a non-evidentialist, non-circular coherence condition can be formulated that permits rational cross-time revision of intention while excluding unexplained drift. The paper would use the Dana case to show that an evidentialist restriction is too narrow, and would develop a positive account—perhaps grounded in planning norms or dynamic rationality—of how revisions can be integrated into an agent's overall psychological backdrop without being evidence-based. The strongest reply to anticipate is that broadening the coherence condition beyond evidentialism severs the link between an agent's earlier intentional states and the predictor's foresight, since unmoored preference shifts would make the psychological trajectory unstable and unpredictable. To succeed, the paper would need to show that its proposed integration condition is independently motivated, does not covertly presuppose the notion of coherence it is meant to explicate, and is still restrictive enough to support the backtracking counterfactuals the original argument requires.

A paper here would argue that the psychological-coherence defense of one-boxing does not stand or fall on permitting non-evidential revision of intention. The evidentialist premise already classifies Dana's unexplained drift as incoherence, so the Dana case does not refute it. The real question is whether the argument needs a permissive condition at all. The paper would show that the choice-prediction link needs only a condition on which later states are coherent with earlier intentions, and that a stricter condition serves the backtracking counterfactuals better. It would then identify the cost of strictness. If the condition is so strict that it classifies reasonable reconsideration as incoherence, then premise c001 excludes agents whose reconsideration is rational, and the conclusion in c012 is restricted to a narrow class of agents. The paper would therefore test candidate conditions against two constraints: they must be strict enough to support the backtracking counterfactuals in c004 and c011, and they must be permissive enough that c001 still describes rational agents. The paper would engage the literature on reconsideration of intention, including Bratman on reasonable non-reconsideration, Holton on resolutions, and McClennen and Gauthier on resolute choice, to distinguish reasonable reconsideration from drift without relying on the premise's evidential classification. It would also assess whether the dispute with two-boxers turns on dominance and on whether backtracking counterfactuals are decision-relevant, rather than on the classification of revision.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The referee is right that the Dana dilemma misreads the premise, since the premise counts drift as incoherence, so the first horn fails. The paper concedes this and drops the claim that the argument needs permissive non-evidential revision. It replies to the remaining worry, that a strict condition is costless, with a dilemma about the definition of rationality. A condition too strict to permit reasonable reconsideration makes c001 false of rational agents and shrinks the scope of c012. A condition loose enough to permit reconsideration weakens the backtracking link. The paper argues that the one-boxer must show a condition that is both strict enough for the link and permissive enough for c001, and it uses the reconsideration literature to locate that condition. Dominance and the decision-relevance of backtracking counterfactuals are treated as the central contested issues.
