## Challenged premise
**W2995194683.c004**: Psychological coherence requires certain backtracking counterfactuals to be true, which entail that choosing one box is the rational decision.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · kimi:moonshotai/Kimi-K2.6*

Alex, a rational agent, faces Newcomb's Problem. Yesterday the predictor scanned Alex's brain and sealed the boxes. Today Alex coherently believes: (1) the predictor is infallible, (2) the million is already physically inside Box B or not, and (3) an extra thousand dollars is better than nothing. Alex's beliefs and desires are psychologically coherent across time, with no contradiction between intention and action. Alex explicitly rejects the backtracking counterfactual 'if I choose two boxes, I must have had a different prior brain state,' instead reasoning: 'My choice now is independent of the already-fixed past scan; taking both boxes simply nets me whatever is in B plus another thousand.' Alex's psychology remains perfectly coherent while denying the required counterfactual and intending to two-box.

## Open questions
- Can a uniformly two-boxing psychology be rendered incoherent by the predictor's nomic success without presupposing the backtracking counterfactual?
- What independent grounds can be given for the extended-process model of decision-making when the standard setup treats the past prediction as fixed?
- Does the defeasibility clause permit evidence that presupposes causal independence, or must coherence constraints override apparent evidence about the fixed past?
- Is the requirement that an agent's whole psychology be explicable as a single extended process a substantive constraint on rationality or a redefinition that begs the question against causal decision theory?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that psychological coherence in Newcomb's Problem does not require the backtracking counterfactuals defended in the original argument, because a uniformly two-boxing psychology can satisfy cross-time coherence constraints without them. The case would develop the objector's observation that a stable two-boxing disposition—where the scan records a two-boxing state, later beliefs and intentions continue it, and the predictor's success is preserved—exhibits no unexplained cross-time disconnect, and that treating the scan as a fixed past event is standard problem setup rather than a question-begging assumption. The strongest reply to anticipate is that genuine coherence requires viewing the decision as an extended process nomically linked to the predictor's scan, so that regarding the choice as causally independent from the prior scan is a bare stance incompatible with the agent's evidence of the predictor's success. To succeed, the paper would need to show that the extended-process model either lacks independent motivation and illicitly presupposes the backtracking counterfactual, or that the standard causal reasoning about the sealed boxes constitutes a legitimate evidence-based defeater that coherence constraints cannot exclude without begging the question.

## Revised after the quality check
*The revision fixes the notion of cross-time coherence, narrows the claim from requiring backtracking counterfactuals to showing that coherence constrains option evaluation only through the agent's account of the scan-choice link, and adds an explicit distinction between forward-tracking and backtracking counterfactuals to answer the infallibility objection.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** Which notion of cross-time psychological coherence is at issue, and can an agent who believes the predictor's success is nomic or infallible, and who deliberates by holding the scan fixed, satisfy that coherence without accepting the backtracking counterfactual from the choice to the scan?

A paper here would argue that the original claim, that psychological coherence requires certain backtracking counterfactuals, is too strong as stated, and that the defensible claim is narrower. Coherence is understood as the absence of contradiction among beliefs, desires, and intentions held across time, together with consistency between intention and action and between the agent's credences and the options it evaluates. On this notion, coherence alone does not force backtracking counterfactuals, because a two-boxing psychology can be internally consistent over time. The paper would then show where the real pressure lies. The belief in nomic or infallible prediction is a belief that the scan and the choice are nomically linked. Whether that link supports counterfactuals from the choice back to the scan, or only forward-tracking counterfactuals from the scan to the prediction, is the substantive question. The paper would argue that nomic success supports forward-tracking counterfactuals from the scan. A coherent agent can therefore accept infallibility while denying that the choice counterfactually fixes the scan, provided it concedes that under its own beliefs a one-box world is one in which the scan differs or the predictor errs. The paper would place this against the backtracking and causal decision theory literature (Lewis, Horgan, Spohn, Joyce), and would argue that the remaining cost is a stability cost, not a cross-time incoherence. The thesis is therefore that coherence constrains evaluation of options only through the agent's account of the scan-choice link, not through a fixed backtracking counterfactual.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The referee is right that infallibility combined with causal independence of the choice from the scan yields a one-box world in which the predictor errs, so Alex cannot both believe infallibility and treat that world as live. The paper accepts this and narrows the thesis accordingly. It distinguishes forward-tracking counterfactuals from the scan (if the scan recorded one-boxing, B would contain the million) from backtracking counterfactuals from the choice (if I one-boxed, the scan would have recorded one-boxing). Nomic success supports only the first. Alex's infallibility belief is then a belief that the actual pair of scan and choice matches, and Alex may reject the second without asserting an erring world, since the denial concerns the choice's causal origin, not the predictor's accuracy. The paper must then show that this stance is not a bare assumption: it must motivate the forward/backward asymmetry independently, and it must concede that the stance leaves the agent with a dispositional instability that the causal decision theory literature has already identified.
