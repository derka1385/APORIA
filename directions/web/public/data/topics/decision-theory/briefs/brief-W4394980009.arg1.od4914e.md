A graduate student, Maria, reads through Pascal's Pensées carefully. In fragment 418, Pascal builds the wager on probabilistic decision theory, locating certainty in expected utility calculations. In fragment 277, he writes 'the heart has its reasons, which reason does not know,' locating certainty in an intuitive, non-rational faculty. In fragment 282, he insists Scripture and church tradition provide the only reliable path, locating certainty in institutional authority. Maria discovers that Pascal himself moves between at least three distinct and competing accounts of where certainty is to be found: calculation, intuition, and revelation. When she asks her seminar which of these Pascal actually endorses, the class splits three ways, each side citing fragments that contradict the others. Pascal never resolves the tension. He is not certain about where certainty lies; he circles the question from different angles without settling it.

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that Pascal's Pensées does not provide the apologetic certainty about the wager's object that the argument requires, because the three fragments identified by the objector present not merely different routes to a single object but competing conceptions of what warrants commitment, with fragment 282's exclusivity undermining the compatibility of calculation and tradition as legitimate paths. The case would examine whether the object-level claims in the remainder of the Pensées genuinely settle whether the seeker wagers on the God of Scripture, the Christian God, or a way of life, or whether the unresolved tension among faculties bleeds into indeterminacy about the object itself. The strongest reply to anticipate is that Pascal's multiple epistemic paths are complementary means of persuasion converging on one unambiguous object, and that the exclusivity in fragment 282 concerns only the reliability of the route, not the identity of the object. To succeed, the paper would need to show that the object-level clarification in the Pensées is robust enough to survive the charge of underdetermination, or alternatively, that the competing routes imply contradictory conditions for what counts as wagering rightly, which would prevent the narrowed premise from doing the work the apologetic argument demands.
