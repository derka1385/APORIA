## Challenged premise
**W4229080938.c012**: If God were not simple, he would depend on properties like power, wisdom, and blessedness as final causes.

## Objection
*reasoner:explorer · anthropic:sonnet*

Consider a being G with two distinct features: power P and wisdom W, with G composite. Suppose G's final cause is the good of exercising P and W together, so G is directed toward a state of affairs that includes both. Now stipulate that P and W are not standing sources that G consults; they are modes of G's own activity, individuated only by what G does. Mapping this onto c012: 'if G were not simple, G would depend on P, W as final causes' requires that each feature be an independent target G must aim at. But here G's end is an activity G itself performs, and the features are specifications of that activity, not external goals. Any composite whose parts are grounded in its own activity can have a final cause that is the whole, so no part is a final cause that G depends on in the sense the antecedent needs. The conditional's consequent fails: composition does not entail dependence on distinct final causes, because the final cause can be the composite's own unified act rather than its constituents.

## Open questions
- Does reconceiving divine attributes as modes of a single act eliminate ontological dependence on distinct constituents, or does the relation of act-specification itself reintroduce dependence?
- Can the revised premise—that composition entails dependence on distinct constituents grounding existence—be defended without assuming that composition requires metaphysical parts with independent being?
- Does the shift from final-cause dependence to ontological grounding preserve the original classical motivation, or does it alter the argument sufficiently to require independent justification?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the standard divine simplicity argument from Plantinga and Ghazālī equivocates between teleological dependence on distinct properties and ontological grounding by distinct constituents. It would develop the objector’s model of a composite whose final cause is its own unified act, showing that composition does not entail dependence on parts as independent final causes. The paper would then argue that the defenders’ retreat to a revised premise about ontological grounding—where distinct constituents secure the composite’s unity—either assumes without argument that act-specifications function as independent metaphysical parts or changes the original subject from final-cause dependence to essential constitution. The strongest reply to anticipate is that even modes of a single act are distinct formal determinations on which the composite’s being relies, so ontological dependence survives the shift from teleology to activity. To succeed, the paper would need to show either that the act-individuation model genuinely dissolves dependence on distinct constituents or that the revised grounding claim cannot be motivated by the classical texts without collapsing into the very composition it seeks to rule out.

## Revised after the quality check
*The revision replaces the claim that act-individuation dissolves dependence with a narrower thesis: composition does not entail final-cause dependence on formally distinct features, so simplicity requires an extra premise excluding real plurality.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** Does the composition-to-dependence inference in c012 hold when the distinct features are only formal distinctions in ratio, with the perfections for whose sake G exists identical to G's own act? If so, which further premise must be added to recover the conclusion that God's existence is simple?

A paper here would argue that premise c012 holds only on a reading of composition that requires real distinctness among the features, and that the act-individuation model does not refute it on the reading that matters for simplicity. The paper would first fix the Avicennan and Ghazālian sense of final cause as a perfection for whose sake a composite exists, not an external target. It would then show that if power and wisdom are formally distinct, plural in ratio but one in reality, the composite is plural in its intelligibility without having a distinct final cause, because the perfection G exists for is G's own act under each description. This concedes the referee's first horn: a model with really distinct determinations reintroduces dependence and so refutes nothing. It accepts the second horn as a reason to narrow the thesis rather than reject it. The paper would argue that c012, as stated, is false on the formal-distinction reading, so the simplicity conclusion cannot be derived from c012 alone. It would then identify the further premise needed, namely that real plurality among the features is excluded, and assess whether the classical texts supply independent grounds for that premise. The paper would not claim that the act model shows God is simple, only that the composition-to-dependence step fails once the distinction is specified.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The paper accepts the dilemma's first horn: if power and wisdom are really distinct determinations, G depends on them and the act-individuation counterexample fails. Against the second horn, it separates formal distinction, which yields plurality in ratio without real composition, from the classical denial of all plurality. A formal distinction does not make the perfections separable parts or grounds of G's being. Against the worry that the whole of a unified act counts as a distinct final cause, the paper holds that under the Avicennan notion the perfection for whose sake G exists is G's own act under each description, so no end distinct from G is required. The result is a narrowed claim: c012 fails on the formal-distinction reading, and simplicity needs a separate argument excluding real plurality.
