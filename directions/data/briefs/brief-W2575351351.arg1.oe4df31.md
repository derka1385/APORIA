## Challenged premise
**W2575351351.c014**: Multiple goods may arise from a single instance of nonresistant nonbelief without increasing that evil.

## Objection
*tradition_lens · anthropic:opus*

Belief in God is properly basic. It arises from a faculty that, working as designed, produces it in ordinary circumstances. Nonresistant nonbelief therefore marks that faculty as dulled, and the dulling is itself the evil. Take Maria, raised secular and sincerely open, whose sense of the divine stays silent. Defenders cite three goods from her single case. The first is a freely undertaken search for God, which needs her faculty dulled only until she begins seeking. The second is solidarity with fellow doubters, which needs it dulled through years of shared uncertainty. The third is moral courage without assurance of divine backing, which needs it fully inert at the moment of her ethical crisis, long after her search began. These goods do not share one fixed evil. Each further good requires the malfunction to run longer, reach deeper, or recur at a new point. A truth-aimed faculty failing for longer and more thoroughly is a larger evil. So stacking goods on her nonbelief enlarges the very evil they are meant to offset.

## Strongest responses and why they failed
- **Defender A**: The goods may arise from one shared interval of nonbelief rather than from separate or progressively deeper malfunctions. Search, solidarity, and moral courage can all be consequences of the same period in which Maria doubts. — *This response did not establish that the listed goods share one bounded interval. It conceded that the original premise needs an explicit restriction to such an episode and a measure of its evil.*
- **Defender B**: Several goods can be consequences of a temporally fixed profile of nonbelief, so counting them separately need not itself lengthen, deepen, or repeat the silence. The relevant comparison should be between that profile and its alternatives. — *The objector argued that God determines the profile rather than inherits it, so the comparison must include the shorter period sufficient for the first good. Defender B conceded that life-spread goods cannot be treated as costless merely because they occur within one biographical episode.*

## Open questions
- What individuates a single episode of nonresistant nonbelief: a continuous period, a fixed psychological profile, or a loss of relationship?
- Should the evil of nonbelief be measured by malfunction in a properly basic belief-forming faculty, by the duration of a lost relationship with God, or by both?
- When goods occur at different times, what makes the longer period of nonbelief necessary for their joint realization rather than merely permitted by it?
- What standard of comparability should govern alternatives: must they realize the same goods, or an adequately comparable set of goods with no greater evil?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the many-goods response requires a comparative, episode-sensitive account of nonresistant nonbelief: several goods may support a single period of hiddenness only when their joint realization requires no additional nonbelief-related evil relative to available alternatives that secure an adequately comparable set of goods. The case would distinguish genuinely overlapping goods from goods distributed across a life and show why merely redescribing an extended deprivation as one instance does not settle its cost. It would anticipate the strongest reply that all goods can be consequences of one fixed interval, and would need to show either that the interval is necessary for the combined goods or that relevant alternatives impose equal or greater evil.

## Revised after the quality check
*The revision replaces the unrestricted possibility claim with an episode-sensitive, nested-versus-distributed criterion, and it supplies a neutral measure of evil and a non-comparative counting target to make the restricted claim non-trivial.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** Can a many-goods response to divine hiddenness count several goods arising from one person's nonresistant nonbelief without treating their joint realization as an increase in nonbelief-related evil, when the measure of that evil does not presuppose a sensus divinitatis account?

A paper here would argue that the many-goods response can count several goods from one person's nonresistant nonbelief without increasing nonbelief-related evil only for goods whose required episodes are nested within a single continuous interval, and that goods requiring separated or non-nested episodes must be booked as additional evil. The argument has three parts. First, it shows that defenders of the many-goods response rely on a non-comparative count, in which everything that happens during one long deprivation is booked against one fixed evil, so that the cost of the deprivation is fixed by the life-span and not by what the goods require. Second, it sets out an episode-individuation criterion: an episode is a maximal continuous interval of nonresistant nonbelief, and a good is nested in an episode when its requirements fall wholly inside that interval. The measure of evil is the total extent of the minimal union of intervals that the joint bundle requires, weighted by a neutral depth index, such as the strength of unresisted absence of conviction, that does not presuppose any account of proper basicality. On this measure nested goods add no further episode, so their joint cost equals the cost of the longest-spanning requirement, which is already a cost on the ledger. Distributed goods that need separate episodes, or that need an interval longer than any single requirement, add measured evil. Third, the paper applies this to Maria's case. Her three goods are nested only to the extent that the search, the shared uncertainty, and the ethical crisis lie in one continuous interval. Where the crisis requires a stretch separated from the search by a period of resisting nonbelief, or where the solidarity good requires recurrence, the paper shows that the good's cost is an added episode rather than a re-description of one. The paper concedes that the modal premise survives only in its restricted, episode-sensitive form, and it anticipates the fixed-interval reply by showing that the interval is necessary for the combined goods only when the goods are nested. Where that condition fails, relevant alternatives that secure comparable goods must be shown to impose equal or greater evil.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The paper does not rest on the Reformed-style claim that dulled faculty is itself the evil. It grants the referee's point that c014 is modal and that comparative necessity is a standard constraint, and it shows that the constraint is non-trivial here because the non-comparative count it targets fails. The count books every good from one deprivation against one fixed evil, which hides how much of the deprivation each good actually requires. The paper replaces this with an episode criterion: nested goods share one episode and add no evil beyond the longest requirement, while distributed or recurrent goods add episodes. The measure uses duration and a neutral depth index rather than the sensus divinitatis premise. This shows that c014 holds for nested goods and fails for distributed ones, so the referee's demand for a non-question-begging measure is met.

*A tidy accounting proposal that, by its own lights, vindicates the modal premise it set out to challenge; it needs to show that the goods doing the real outweighing are non-nested before it becomes an objection rather than a footnote.*

- Strongest remaining objection: c014 is an existential modal claim: multiple goods *may* arise from one instance without increasing the evil. The paper's own criterion grants that nested goods sharing one continuous interval add no evil beyond the longest requirement. That establishes the possibility c014 asserts, and c021 is likewise only a 'may'. So the episode-sensitive restriction leaves the argument's modal conclusion intact. The defender simply picks a nested bundle, and the paper refutes nothing the argument claims. It only shows that some bundles (Maria's, under some descriptions) would be costed differently.
- What it still needs: The paper must show that the goods that do the collective outweighing in c017 (the goods actually cited, not invented nested ones) are typically non-nested, so that restricting to nested goods drains the many-goods response of its aggregate value. It must also defend the interval-additive, depth-weighted measure against a relational view of the evil that is not additive over time.
