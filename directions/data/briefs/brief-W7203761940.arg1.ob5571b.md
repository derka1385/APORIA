A bridge inspector finds deep corrosion in the supports. He believes closing the bridge will embarrass the mayor before a festival, so he hides the report. The mayor, who has ordered inspections shortened to save money, approves the bridge’s use. During the festival, the bridge collapses. The inspector’s daughter, whom he had urged to attend, dies on it; the mayor’s son, whom the mayor kept home because of a private warning, survives. The collapse has identifiable causes: corrosion, the inspector’s concealment, and the mayor’s policy. Its outcome also has a visible moral pattern: those endangered are connected to the officials’ choices, while protection follows the mayor’s preferential use of power. The suffering is terrible, but it is neither morally indiscriminate nor non-monotonic in this case.

## Open questions
- What evidential method could determine whether outcomes tracking culpability, desert, privilege, or power are systematically dominant across history?
- How should morally intelligible patterns generated by human agency be distinguished from evidence of moral governance or providential direction?
- What degree and kind of correspondence between character and outcome would count as a directional signature of history being steered toward the good?
- Can the absence of a benevolent directional signature underdetermine theism and naturalism if history nevertheless displays recurrent non-benevolent moral patterns?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the argument from worldly randomness requires an explicit and independently supported aggregate premise about the distribution of morally intelligible outcomes in history. It would show that individual cases of harm structured by culpability, privilege, and power neither establish providence nor simply count as morally indiscriminate, while defending the claim that no reliable benevolent directional signature follows from their recurrence. The strongest reply to anticipate is that the relevant conclusion needs only inconsistency, not universal indiscriminacy; the paper would need to specify a standard for identifying moral patterns, justify the move from cases to an aggregate assessment, and explain why the resulting evidential profile underdetermines the rival worldviews.

Its load-bearing premises are symmetry and absence: naturalism and biblical theology each explain apparent randomness through their own resources (impersonal causation and probability on one side; agency, natural processes, hidden providence, and delayed judgment on the other), and historical evidence lacks the directional signature a benevolently steered history would be expected to show. The paper would then state a principled criterion separating morally legible patterns produced by human agency from patterns relevant to providence. A pattern counts as human-agency-produced when it is fully accounted for by the culpability, privilege, and power of agents in the causal chain. A pattern counts as providence-relevant only if it displays desert-sensitive distribution that is not exhausted by those agents' choices. The bridge case illustrates the first category: the inspector's concealment and the mayor's policy fully account for who died and who was spared, so it is accommodated by both worldviews and does not discriminate between them. The symmetry claim also confuses accommodation with evidential parity. If c038 and c039 empty theism of predictions about earthly distribution, then 'both explain randomness' says nothing about likelihoods. The proposed criterion has a further problem. It must also replace bare 'both worldviews explain it' with a comparative likelihood analysis that engages Draper and the skeptical-theism literature. Finally, the criterion separating agency-produced from providence-relevant patterns has to be defended against double-agency views of providence.
