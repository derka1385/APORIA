## Revised after the quality check
*The revision drops the temperament-based tiered model, defines the postmodern rendition from textual sources, and replaces the certainty-versus-ambiguity contrast with a non-preference criterion that separates uncertainty about existence from uncertainty about the wager's object.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** Under what non-preference-based criterion is ambiguity an apologetic defect, such that Pascal's wager should be preferred to a postmodern rendition of it, given that the wager itself already reasons under uncertainty about God's existence?

A paper here would argue that the conditional premise (if the postmodern rendition is ambivalent, fails to give the seeker certainty and hope, and Pascal fixes the object of the wager, then Pascal's wager should be used in apologetics) is valid only with an unstated bridge: that apologetics is answerable to the epistemic and practical ends the wager sets, not to the seeker's temperament. The paper would first identify the postmodern rendition from its textual sources, such as the works of Caputo, Vattimo and Kearney the referee names, and state precisely which claims about it are being attributed. It would then separate two things the original argument runs together: the epistemic status of the wager's premises (whether the probability of God's existence is known) and the psychological certainty or hope the argument is meant to produce in a seeker. Pascal's wager already accepts uncertainty about whether God exists, so ambiguity about existence cannot by itself be the defect. The criterion the paper defends is an object-level one. Ambiguity is an apologetic defect when it leaves the outcome matrix undefined, that is, when the seeker cannot say what is being wagered on, because the dominance comparison that makes the wager action-guiding requires a determinate object. Ambiguity about probability is compatible with the wager's reasoning; ambiguity about the object is not. On this criterion the postmodern rendition is defective only if its ambivalence reaches the object, and the paper would test that claim against the texts rather than against Emma's preference. The thesis is therefore narrowed: it does not claim that Pascal's wager should replace the postmodern rendition for seekers who value honesty, but that Pascal's wager is preferable for apologetics only where the postmodern rendition's ambiguity is shown to be object-level, and that uncertainty about existence is no objection to the wager at all.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The paper concedes that individual taste is irrelevant to the normative question and that the wager presupposes uncertainty, so it drops the claim that Pascal offers certainty against the postmodern ambiguity of 'embracing uncertainty'. It replies with a distinction between uncertainty about whether God exists and uncertainty about what is wagered on. The wager tolerates the first, since its dominance reasoning is run on probabilities. It does not tolerate the second, since the comparison of outcomes needs a determinate object. The criterion is non-preferential: ambiguity is a defect when it makes the wager's outcome matrix undefined. The paper then tests whether the postmodern rendition's ambivalence falls on the object or only on the probability, using the textual sources. Only the first would count against it. The distinction between epistemic status and psychological certainty is applied throughout, so the premise is evaluated by what the wager can establish, not by what a seeker feels.

*The epistemic/psychological and probability/object distinctions are a sensible start, but as planned the paper applies a decision-theoretic standard to texts that disclaim it and ignores the many-gods problem facing Pascal's own object, so it needs substantial rethinking before it is a defensible paper.*

- Strongest remaining objection: The object-level criterion begs the question against the postmodern rendition and does not favour Pascal. Caputo's 'religion without religion', Vattimo's weak thought and Kearney's anatheism do not offer a decision-theoretic wager with an outcome matrix. They reject that framing, so judging them by whether the matrix is defined imposes Pascal's standard on a rival that does not accept it. And Pascal's own matrix is not determinate either. The many-gods and mixed-strategy objections (Cargile, Hájek, Jordan) show that fixing the object as the Christian God, by appeal to the rest of the Pensées, does not settle the partition of states that dominance reasoning needs. So the criterion either convicts both renditions or neither.
- What it still needs: The paper must show that the postmodern texts claim to be wagers at all, so that the outcome-matrix test is not a category mistake. It must also show that Pascal's rendition meets the determinacy criterion despite the many-gods partition problem; otherwise the criterion cannot ground a comparative preference.
