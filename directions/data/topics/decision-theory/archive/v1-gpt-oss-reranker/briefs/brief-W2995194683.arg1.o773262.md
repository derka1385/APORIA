## Argument — Coherence-based One-Boxing Argument
From: The philosopher’s paradox: How to make a coherent decision in the Newcomb Problem (`oa:W2995194683`)

- **W2995194683.c001**: A rational person's intentional states are psychologically coherent across time, and rational decisions are made against this coherent backdrop.
- **W2995194683.c002**: Decisions are extended processes like a golf swing; their coherence with other psychological states links the choice to the predictor's prediction.
- **W2995194683.c004**: Psychological coherence requires certain backtracking counterfactuals to be true, which entail that choosing one box is the rational decision.
- **W2995194683.c009**: When the predictor's success is nomic, it provides counterfactual support for the prediction.

- **Therefore W2995194683.c012**: Once rationality is understood in terms of psychological coherence, the rational decision in Newcomb's Problem is to choose one box.

## Challenged premise
**W2995194683.arg1.r1**: A rational agent's later intentional states need only be coherent with her earlier ones in the sense that any conflict is explained by evidence acquired since the earlier state was formed, so that apparent cross-time conflicts are defeasible revisions rather than incoherence.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · anthropic:sonnet*

Dana forms an intention on Monday to finish her thesis by June, and plans her spring around it. In March she receives evidence that her advisor will leave, making the thesis impossible to finish in time. She now intends to abandon it. Her later state conflicts with her earlier one, and the revised premise classifies this as a defeasible revision, since the conflict is explained by new evidence. But suppose Dana never learns the advisor's departure at all. Instead, in March she simply finds the thesis unappealing and drops the goal, with no new information bearing on whether June is achievable. Her earlier intention and her later state still conflict, yet nothing acquired since Monday explains the change. The revised premise says apparent cross-time conflicts are revisions only when evidence accounts for them, so it classifies Dana's shift as incoherence, which the premise itself rules out as rational-coherent. The premise therefore cannot state the coherence condition it needs: it either counts Dana's unexplained drift as coherent, which it was not meant to, or it denies that rational agents ever change course without evidence, which the argument does not license.

## Open questions
- How can ‘integrated revision’ be formally distinguished from ‘unexplained drift’ without invoking circular definitions?
- What normative criteria determine which rational pressures (evidential, preferential, etc.) legitimately explain cross‑temporal conflicts?
- Does the revised coherence condition preserve the necessary link between decision coherence and the predictor’s success in Newcomb’s Problem?
- Can the extended‑process view of decisions be reconciled with standard decision‑theoretic models while maintaining a robust coherence requirement?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the central obstacle to the Newcomb‑Problem argument is the lack of a precise, non‑circular coherence condition for rational agents' cross‑temporal intentional states. It would first reconstruct the original coherence premise and the objections concerning unexplained drift, then develop a formal distinction between integrated revisions—changes that are assimilated into an overall plan—and mere drift, drawing on theories of plan revision and intention coherence. Anticipating Defender A’s reply, the paper would show how to articulate the integration clause without circularity by grounding it in a hierarchy of intentions and plan‑maintenance mechanisms. It would also address Defender B’s broader premise by specifying the class of rational pressures that count as legitimate explanations, perhaps via a normative account of preference stability and evidential updating. Finally, the paper would demonstrate that the refined coherence condition still supports the causal‑psychological link to the predictor required for the Newcomb conclusion, thereby preserving the original argument while resolving the identified gap.

## Revised after the quality check
*The revision narrows the thesis from claiming that a refined drift/revision distinction is the central obstacle to the Newcomb argument to a self-standing account of reconsideration norms, with the link to one-boxing treated as a tested conditional rather than an asserted conclusion.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** Which reconsideration norm separates legitimate change in an agent's intentions over time from unexplained drift, and does a coherence condition built on that norm entail the backtracking counterfactuals the Newcomb argument needs, or does the link to one-boxing fail even when coherence is granted?

A paper here would argue that the premise that a rational agent's later intentional states must cohere with her earlier ones is best stated as a norm on reconsideration. A later conflict is permissible when it is a reasons-responsive revision, grounded in evidence or in a practical consideration the agent can cite and actually weighs. It is impermissible when it is unexplained drift. The paper would first show that the original premise, whose explanation clause admits only evidence, cannot classify value-based reconsideration correctly. The Dana case exposes this: the operative dividing line is between reasons-responsive change and change with no reason-bearing basis, not between evidence-driven and evidence-free change. The paper would then state this norm precisely enough to be tested. It would treat the Newcomb link as a separate, explicit claim rather than a consequence of the norm. It would argue that cross-temporal coherence bears on Newcomb only if the condition constrains the relation between the choice and the prediction, and it would test whether any formulation of the norm entails the backtracking counterfactuals in the argument. It would concede that a causal decision theorist can accept the coherence norm while rejecting those counterfactuals, and it would identify the exact premise a two-boxer must reject for the bridge to fail. If no formulation of the norm forces the backtracking counterfactuals, the paper's conclusion is that the Newcomb argument's coherence step is not secured by the reconsideration norm. The paper would then stand as a self-contained account of reconsideration norms, with the Newcomb application reported as a conditional result.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The paper accepts the referee's central point: refining the drift/revision distinction does not by itself move the CDT/EDT dispute. It therefore no longer presents that refinement as the central obstacle to the Newcomb argument. It first answers the dilemma. The original premise does not count drift as coherent. Its defect is that its explanation clause has no way to say why unexplained change is a failure, since it admits only evidence as explanation. The paper replaces that clause with a reasons-responsiveness clause, which permits value-based reconsideration and excludes bare drift, using the distinction between a change the agent can cite as a reason and a change with no reason-bearing basis. For the Newcomb link, the paper does not assert the bridge. It states the premise the bridge requires and checks whether a two-boxer can accept the coherence norm while rejecting the backtracking counterfactuals. If they can, the paper reports that result and does not claim the link.
