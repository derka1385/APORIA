## Challenged premise
**W2124966002.arg1.r1**: Faith on the Jamesian model can be assurance-bearing while remaining a venture, because the risk is borne in the act of committing before proof and the felt certitude is a subsequent state of the committed believer, not a bracketing of the contingency that constituted the venture.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · anthropic:sonnet*

Consider Marta, a hospice chaplain who commits to trusting that her dying patients meet a loving God. Her commitment is made before any proof, with real risk: she could be wrong, and she knows it. Two years later, after many deathbeds, she feels settled, unshakeable certitude that God is there. The premise says this certitude is a later state that leaves the venture intact. But her certitude now treats the contingency as closed: she no longer experiences the belief as something she might lose or that could have turned out otherwise. When she prays, she does not feel herself wagering. The risk she bore has been converted into a certainty she takes to be evidence-independent. So the felt assurance is not a subsequent state standing beside the venture; it is the venture's risk dissolved from the inside, and the premise's distinction between the act and the state cannot be drawn in her case.

## Strongest responses and why they failed
- **Defender A**: The risk is borne in the committing act and the later certitude is a subsequent state; the venture persists because the original act was made under live contingency, and the distinction between act and state remains drawable via a counterfactual test. — *The counterfactual test is weak—if the believer’s certitude treats the ground as closed, her “would still commit” answer is idle, so the test cannot separate a sustained venture from a conviction that has absorbed the risk, and the added answerability condition is not supplied by James.*
- **Defender A**: Revises the premise to say the risk is fixed at the time of commitment and the later felt certitude need not display that risk, so the venture’s occurrence does not depend on later introspective access. — *Although it avoids the answerability clause, it still offers no way to empirically or phenomenologically distinguish a venture that endures from a conviction that has merged with the original risk, leaving the core objection unresolved.*
- **Defender B**: Clarifies that the venture remains because the original act of committing under epistemic risk is constitutive, and subsequent assurance may obscure the risk without dissolving the venture’s structure. — *The reply does not engage the objection that the believer’s self‑understanding treats the risk as eliminated; without a clear criterion for when risk is merely obscured versus erased, the premise remains vulnerable.*

## Open questions
- How can one reliably discriminate, from the inside of a believer’s experience, whether a later certitude merely obscures or actually eliminates the original epistemic risk?
- Does the Jamesian model require an answerability or revisability condition for the later state, and if so, is that condition philosophically justified?
- What is the ontological status of the original risk after a belief hardens into settled conviction within the doxastic‑venture framework?
- Can a phenomenological account capture the persistence of risk independent of the believer’s introspective access to it?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the Jamesian doxastic‑venture model must be refined to include a structural account of the original risk that survives independently of later phenomenology, while also providing a clear, testable criterion for when a later assurance merely obscures versus when it dissolves that risk. It would begin by reconstructing the original argument and the objector’s case study of Marta, then critically assess Defender A’s counterfactual and answerability revisions and Defender B’s claim that risk can be merely hidden. Anticipating the strongest reply—that risk is constitutive and persists regardless of introspective access—the paper would need to demonstrate that such a claim either entails an implicit revisability condition or fails to explain the believer’s epistemic self‑understanding, and would propose a revised premise that ties the venture’s persistence to an external, perhaps normative, standard rather than to internal phenomenology alone.

**Revised question:** On Bishop's Jamesian model, does a doxastic venture survive a believer's later evidence-independent certitude when the believer no longer feels any risk, and if it does, can the venture component contribute to the phenomenological superiority claimed in c047?

A paper here would argue that, on Bishop's Jamesian model, whether a belief is a doxastic venture is fixed by a standing relation between the belief and total evidence, not by the believer's felt sense of risk. So Marta's loss of the sense of wagering does not by itself end the venture. The paper would isolate the threatening reading that the original proposal missed: Marta's deathbed experiences either are evidence that closes the evidential gap on p, in which case her belief ceases to be a venture and becomes an experience-based belief, or they are not evidence bearing on p, in which case the venture persists but its riskiness is inaccessible to her and so cannot explain her assurance. The paper defends the second horn as the thesis: a venture wholly inaccessible to the believer cannot do the phenomenological work that the superiority conclusion requires, so the Jamesian account cannot be preferred on phenomenological grounds. It drops both the demand for an introspective criterion and the appeal to an external normative standard. It tests the dilemma against Bishop's Believing by Faith and the faith-and-risk literature on Buchak, Howard-Snyder and McKaughan. Its central claim is that the venture's persistence is a fact about evidential relations, while any phenomenological advantage must come from something the believer can register.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The referee is right that felt risk is not the venture. On Bishop's model the venture is a standing relation between believing p and total evidence, and Bishop allows full certitude, so the introspective criterion and the external standard are both dropped and the paper does not rest on the act/state distinction in the premise. The paper accepts persistence and turns to the dilemma the referee's reading exposes. If Marta's deathbed experiences are evidence closing the gap, the belief is no longer a venture and the case is experience-based.
