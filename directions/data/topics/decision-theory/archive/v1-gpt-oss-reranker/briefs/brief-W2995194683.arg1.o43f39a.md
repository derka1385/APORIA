## Challenged premise
**W2995194683.c001**: A rational person's intentional states are psychologically coherent across time, and rational decisions are made against this coherent backdrop.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · mistral:mistralai/Mistral-Medium-3.5-128B*

Alice is a rational scientist who, at T1, intends to accept a high-paying job if offered by T2. At T2, she is offered the job but, due to new information about unethical practices, decides to reject it. Her intentional states are internally consistent at each time, and her decision is rational given her updated beliefs. Yet her psychological states are not coherent *across time*—her initial intention and later decision conflict—without irrationality.

## Strongest responses and why they failed
- **Defender A**: The argument's c001 says a rational agent's intentional states are coherent across time, not that every later decision must match every earlier intention. Alice's case is a revision: her T1 intention was formed on beliefs she later rationally abandoned, so the coherence the argument needs is coherence of the backdrop against which the decision is made, including the updated beliefs that the T1 intention was conditional on. Read that way, Alice's states are coherent across time, because the T2 rejection is what the T1 intention, plus new evidence, would itself endorse. The objection bites only if coherence is read as strict persistence of a formed intention, and the argument does not say that. Still, the objection exposes a real gap: c001 does not say what makes a revision coherent rather than a lapse. Without a criterion, 'coherent across time' is too permissive to do the work c012 needs, since almost any change could be labelled rational revision. — *It sidesteps the original claim by redefining coherence as rational responsiveness to new evidence, but offers no independent justification or criterion, leaving the premise overly permissive and failing to protect the argument's inference to one-boxing.*
- **Defender A**: You are right that my previous reply changed the premise rather than defending it. c001 says coherence, and Alice's T1 intention and T2 rejection are a genuine cross-time conflict that she rightly resolves. Calling her revision 'coherent' only works if coherence is redefined as responsiveness, and that redefinition makes c001 nearly empty. The argument's own c018 links rationality to reasons cohering with a person's intentional states, and Alice's case shows that a rational agent can have conflicting states across time. So the premise as stated does not survive. The argument still needs some cross-time constraint to connect the decision to the predictor's forecast via c002 and c035, so the repair is to weaken c001 rather than drop the link. Revised premise: A rational agent's later intentional states need only be coherent with her earlier ones in the sense that any conflict is explained by evidence acquired since the earlier state was formed, so that apparent cross-time conflicts are defeasible revisions rather than incoherence. — *Although it acknowledges the failure of the original premise, it merely proposes a weakened, defeasible version without supplying a concrete, non‑circular account of when a revision counts as coherent, leaving the premise insufficient for the later steps of the argument.*
- **Defender B**: You are right that if [W2995194683.c001] forbids any diachronic change in intentional content, Alice is a rational counterexample. The argument does not need such a strong claim. The predictor's forecast relies on the participant's rational psychological makeup determining both the prediction and the choice, and some intentional states can reliably predict later decisions without undermining autonomy. Alice's T2 decision coheres with her updated beliefs, and the underlying disposition to respond to evidence is itself a stable, coherent feature of her psychology. Because [W2995194683.c001] is phrased as a blanket claim about all intentional states across time, your objection lands partly. I concede the premise must be narrowed to preserve the argument. Revised premise: A rational person's underlying rational psychological makeup is coherent across time, and rational decisions are made against this stable backdrop, even when particular intentions are rationally revised in light of new evidence. — *The proposed narrowing shifts focus to a vague notion of 'underlying rational psychological makeup' without specifying how it grounds the required diachronic link between the agent's intentions and the predictor's forecast, so the revised premise remains under‑determined.*

A paper here would argue that the one-boxing conclusion of the Newcomb argument is licensed only by a narrower claim than the one originally proposed. Coherence across time cannot, by itself, settle whether dominance or causal expected utility should guide choice, so the paper drops the claim that coherence alone secures one-boxing. The coherence premise (c001) is restated without strict persistence of prior intentions: a rational agent's intentional states must form a process in which earlier stages (intentions, dispositions, deliberative commitments) are not evidentially or practically severed from the act that terminates them. The Alice case is reassigned to its proper role. It shows that a coherent agent may revise an intention when evidence arrives, but Newcomb contains no such evidence, so the paper treats Alice only as a contrast case and not as the source of the premise. The central thesis is conditional. If the choice is the terminal stage of an extended process (c002) and the predictor's success is nomic (c009), then a coherent agent who treats her own deliberation as part of the process that the predictor foresees (c011) must evaluate options with the counterfactuals that track that process, which are backtracking, not the causal counterfactuals that hold the box contents fixed. The paper engages the causal and evidential decision theory literature (Joyce, Ahmed, Spohn, Arntzenius) and the intention-stability literature (Bratman, Gauthier) to show exactly where this conditional departs from them. The formal criterion is specified as follows: a choice is coherent with prior intentions just in case the counterfactual supported by the process, not by the fixed-state dominance argument, is the one the agent must use to assess her options. The paper then proves that, under nomic prediction, this criterion makes the backtracking counterfactuals in c004 through c012 the decision-relevant ones. The paper does not claim to refute causal decision theory. It claims that the two-boxer must deny that the choice is the terminus of the same process whose coherence the premise describes, and it argues that this denial is costly.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The referee is right that stable, coherent dispositions and a tracking predictor are compatible with the two-boxer's claim that box contents are fixed at choice time. The paper accepts this and does not claim otherwise. Its reply is that the two-boxer's dominance argument treats the choice as an act severable from the extended process in which the disposition is realised (c002). Under the process reading, the 'fixed at the moment of choice' premise is not neutral: it assumes the choice point is independent of the earlier stages that the predictor tracks. The paper argues that a coherent agent cannot make that assumption while also holding that her deliberation is the same process the predictor foresees under nomic success (c009, c011). The distinction it uses is between the agent's standing dispositions, which the predictor tracks, and the causal independence of the act, which dominance requires. The paper argues that coherence favours the first as the decision-relevant counterfactual basis. On the Alice point, revision on new evidence is idle in Newcomb, so the paper does not rely on it.
