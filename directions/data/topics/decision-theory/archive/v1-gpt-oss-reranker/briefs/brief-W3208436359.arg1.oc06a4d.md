## Challenged premise

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · anthropic:sonnet*

Ann must choose between Gamble X and Gamble Y. Both pay only finite amounts, and both have finite expected utility of exactly 5. X pays 5 for certain. Y pays 5 with probability 1, plus, with probability 1/ω (an infinitesimal), an additional payoff of 1 to the agent. Finitist EU theory counts the two options as tied, so it permits Ann to pick either. Every payoff and probability she faces is either finite or infinitesimal, and all of her expected utilities are finite reals. A surreal-framed theory computes Y's expectation as 5 + 1/ω, so it ranks Y strictly above X. That verdict compares two options whose finitist expectations are both 5, so the surreal theory issues a strict preference the finitist theory does not, and the extension is not conservative over finitist decision claims.

## Strongest responses and why they failed
- **Defender A**: The objection assumes the finitist theory has a verdict on Ann's choice, but Y involves a probability of 1/ω, which finitist EU theory cannot represent. Conservativity constrains only claims statable in the base language, so a strict surreal preference over Y and X is not a new finitist claim. The fair pressure point is the scope of the premise. The paper's claim is that the surreal theory agrees with finitist EU on finite lotteries with real probabilities, and your example falls outside that domain. — *It shows the objection misapplies finitist EU to a gamble it cannot express, but it concedes that the original premise was overly broad and needs scope restriction.*
- **Defender B**: You assume finitist EU theory can represent Gamble Y, but it cannot: 1/ω is not a standard real probability, so finitist theory collapses that component to zero and misrepresents the gamble rather than rendering a genuine verdict. The conservative extension claim holds over the domain both theories share: finite lotteries with standard real probabilities and finite utilities, where surreal expectations reduce to real ones and preserve every finitist preference. Your case involves a non‑Archimedean probability outside that domain; showing that surreal theory discriminates structures invisible to finitist theory is precisely what a successful extension should do. — *It correctly limits conservativity to the shared domain, but it leaves open how the surreal framework can be justified as a foundation for the transfinite cases the paper aims to address.*
- **Defender A**: Conservativity is a claim about what the base theory can say: it guarantees that surreal‑framed theory adds no new verdicts on claims statable in finitist EU language. Your Gamble Y is not such a claim, because its probability 1/ω is not a finitist object. So a strict surreal preference over Y and X is an extension, not a violation. The transfinite work is done by the surreal representation, not by conservativity, and c062 supports the representation theorem only for finite lotteries with the stated continuity and independence conditions. — *It acknowledges the limitation of conservativity but does not resolve the broader issue of providing a firm foundation for transfinite decision problems.*

A paper here would argue that the substantive question for a surreal-number decision theory is representation, not conservativity. It would take as its object of study the class of finite-support lotteries whose probabilities and payoffs are surreal, and would state a small set of preference axioms over such lotteries, including completeness, transitivity, a surreal-valued independence condition, and a dominance condition. It would then ask for a representation theorem: under which of these axioms is there a surreal-valued probability and utility assignment whose expectation represents the preference, and is that assignment unique up to the admissible transformations. The paper would compare this construction with the lexicographic and non-Archimedean utility representations of Hausner and Fishburn, with the treatment of infinitesimal probabilities in Hajek and Pruss, and with the relative-utility proposal of Bartha, showing where the surreal approach gives a representation those accounts lack and where it gives none.
