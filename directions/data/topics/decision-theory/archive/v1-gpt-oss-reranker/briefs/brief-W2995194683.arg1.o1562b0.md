## Challenged premise
**W2995194683.arg1.r3**: A rational agent's intentional states include standing counterfactual dispositions toward the predictor's model of her, so that a belief in the predictor's infallibility for her own choices requires the backtracking counterfactual that her choice would have differed had the scan differed.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · glm:zai-org/GLM-5.2*

Maya, a statistician, faces Newcomb's Problem. She believes the predictor is infallible based solely on its published 1000/1000 track record. She has never been told she was scanned, knows nothing about any model of her, and has no beliefs or dispositions concerning the predictor's internal representation of her. Her decision procedure is straightforward expected-utility maximization: given infallibility, one-boxing yields $1M and two-boxing yields $1000, so she one-boxes. Had the predictor's scan produced different data—say, due to a sensor glitch—Maya would still have run the identical expected-utility calculation from the identical track record and one-boxed. Her choice is causally insensitive to the scan's content; it depends only on the publicly available success rate. She is fully rational, her intentional states are coherent, and she believes the predictor is infallible, yet no backtracking counterfactual connects her choice to the scan's output.

## Open questions

- Is the requirement that agents view the predictor's success as causally grounded in their psychology necessary for the coherence argument, or can a purely statistical belief be accommodated?
- What methodological moves are needed to derive the revised premise from standard decision‑theoretic or epistemic principles?
- Can the revised premise be defended against agents who have no access to, or no concept of, the predictor's scanning process?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the coherence‑based solution to Newcomb's Problem must be reformulated to distinguish between agents whose belief in a predictor's infallibility is grounded in a causal model of their own psychology and those whose belief rests solely on statistical evidence. It would reconstruct the original argument, isolate the point at which the backtracking counterfactual is invoked, and anticipate Defender A's and Defender B's revised premises as the strongest replies. The paper would then show that without a principled account of how such a causal representation is generated—perhaps via a normative requirement of evidential integration—the revised premises remain insufficient to guarantee the necessary counterfactual link, leaving the coherence argument incomplete.

## Revised after the quality check
*The thesis shifts from a causal-model versus statistical grounding distinction, which the backtracking reading undercuts, to a narrower claim that the standing-disposition clause is dispensable and that coherence is sufficient but not necessary for one-boxing.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** Does a rational agent's belief in a predictor's infallibility, under whatever grounding it has, entail the backtracking counterfactual that her choice would have differed had the scan differed, and if so, is that counterfactual doing the work in securing one-boxing, or is one-boxing secured by a route that does not pass through psychological coherence?

A paper here would argue that the coherence argument for one-boxing in Newcomb's Problem is sound only in a narrower form than originally stated. The original premise ties the backtracking counterfactual to standing counterfactual dispositions toward the predictor's model of the agent. The Maya case shows that this clause is dispensable. Read as a forward, interventionist counterfactual, the glitch scenario is the wrong test, since it holds Maya's psychology fixed while altering the scan, and stipulating a misread scan conflicts with the infallibility she believes in. The right test is backtracking: hold the predictor's reliability fixed and ask whether Maya's choice would have differed had the scan differed. On that reading, infallibility held fixed already entails that the scan covaries with the choice, because the prediction must match the choice. So the counterfactual is supported by the content of the infallibility belief itself, whatever its grounding. The paper therefore concedes the referee's point that statistical grounding does not undermine backtracking support, and drops the claim that a causal-model versus statistical distinction is needed. What survives is a claim about the structure of the argument. Coherence, understood as the backtracking counterfactual plus infallibility, is sufficient for one-boxing. It is not necessary, because Maya one-boxes by expected-utility maximization on the track record alone, with no self-directed counterfactual dispositions. The paper argues that this shows the coherence route secures one-boxing only for agents whose belief in infallibility is represented as covering their own choices, and that the general conclusion c012 must be restated accordingly. It does not defend the claim that statistical belief fails to support the relevant counterfactuals, since on the backtracking reading it does not fail. The normative requirement of evidential integration is dropped as unspecified. The paper's contribution is to isolate which premise carries the load, to show that the glitch objection targets an equivocation rather than the conclusion, and to specify the backtracking semantics needed for the argument to go through.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The paper accepts the referee's diagnosis. The glitch case is a forward, interventionist counterfactual, and it stipulates a predictor error, which conflicts with infallibility. The paper therefore does not rely on it. It reformulates the test as backtracking: holding the predictor's reliability fixed, the question is whether Maya's choice would have differed had the scan differed. Infallibility, read as a belief about the match between prediction and choice, supplies this covariation whatever its grounding, so the case does not refute the backtracking counterfactual. The paper also accepts that Maya one-boxes, so the case does not threaten c012 for her. What it does threaten is the claim that coherence through standing dispositions toward the predictor's model is what secures one-boxing. The paper restricts the coherence argument to agents whose infallibility belief covers their own choices, and it notes that expected-utility reasoning on the track record is a separate, sufficient route.
