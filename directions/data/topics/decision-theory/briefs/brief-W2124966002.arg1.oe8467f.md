## Challenged premise
**W2124966002.c031**: The Jamesian doxastic‑venture model supplies what is needed by treating faith as a passionally based tendency to believe and as believingly accepting an unestablished claim.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · mistral:mistralai/Mistral-Medium-3.5-128B*

A person, Alex, passionally wants to believe their severely ill child will recover, and thus forms a tendency to believe this unestablished claim. Yet Alex does not accept the claim as true—they oscillate between hope and despair, never settling into belief. The Jamesian model here fails to produce believing acceptance, only an unstable tendency.

## Open questions
- What distinguishes a completed, stabilizing doxastic venture from an unstable or withdrawn attempt at acceptance?
- Can the Jamesian model maintain its phenomenological superiority if faith is restricted to cases where belief has already stabilized?
- Is the stability of faith a causal result of volitional commitment, or a separate doxastic condition that the model must independently specify?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the Jamesian doxastic-venture model cannot substantiate its claim to phenomenological superiority over sub-doxastic rivals unless it provides a non-circular account of how a volitional act of believing acceptance produces a settled disposition rather than merely coexisting with psychological oscillation. The thesis would be developed by examining whether the stability required for assurance is a constitutive feature of the venture itself or an independent psychological achievement, drawing on cases where agents sincerely attempt acceptance yet remain doxastically unsettled. The strongest reply to anticipate is that the model distinguishes successful ventures from incomplete pre-venturesome states, so that oscillation simply marks the absence of genuine faith. The paper would need to show that this reply renders the account either empirically unfalsifiable—since any instability is dismissed as not-yet-venturesome—or incomplete, because it leaves the transition from tendency to stable assurance unexplained and therefore fails to demonstrate that the Jamesian model uniquely captures the phenomenology of Christian faith.

## Revised after the quality check
*The revision drops the mechanism-of-stabilization and unfalsifiability charges and reframes the thesis as a gap between believing acceptance and assurance or certitude that undermines the phenomenological superiority claim in c047.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** Does the Jamesian doxastic-venture model's account of believing acceptance, understood as taking an unestablished claim as true in practical reasoning while remaining compatible with doubt, deliver the assurance or certitude that the model needs in order to claim phenomenological superiority over sub-doxastic rivals?

A paper here would argue that the Jamesian doxastic-venture model's constitutive analysis of faith (a passional tendency to believe plus believing acceptance of an unestablished claim) can be granted as a conceptual account of what faith is, but that the inference from this analysis to the claim that the model captures the assurance or certitude of Christian faith (c047) fails. The argument proceeds by separating believing acceptance, understood as taking p as true in practical reasoning under evidential ambiguity and compatible with doubt, from assurance, understood as the absence of live doubt and felt settledness about p. Using Bishop's account of believing by faith and the Howard-Snyder and McKaughan literature on doubt-compatible faith, the paper would show that an agent can satisfy every conjunct of the model while retaining the doubt that assurance excludes, so the model does not by itself deliver the phenomenology cited in c047. The paper would then assess whether sub-doxastic and attenuated rivals fare any better on the same measure. The paper drops any demand that the model supply a psychological mechanism for stabilization, and drops the unfalsifiability charge, since both misread a constitutive analysis. Its comparative claim is restricted to what the model's own conditions entail about assurance, and it leaves open whether a separate account of certitude could be added to the venture model.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The referee is right that Alex lacks the second conjunct, so the Alex vignette does not refute c031 as a constitutive analysis, and the paper concedes this. The reply instead locates the defect in the step from c031 to c047. The paper distinguishes believing acceptance (taking p as true in practical reasoning while doubt remains) from the assurance or certitude that c047 requires. Believing acceptance as defined is satisfied by doubt-compatible acceptance, so meeting the model's conditions does not secure the phenomenology of assurance. This removes the mechanism demand and the unfalsifiability charge. On parity, the paper does not assert a comparative weakness peculiar to the venture model. It asks whether any rival delivers assurance on the same measure, and it limits the superiority claim to what the model's own conditions entail.
