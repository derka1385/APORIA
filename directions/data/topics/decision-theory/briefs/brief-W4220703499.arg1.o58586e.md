## Challenged premise

## Objection
*tradition_lens · gemma:google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it*

Imagine a man named Elias who chooses to drink water at noon. According to the Grounding Principle, the truth of this choice and the knowledge of it are grounded in Elias's act. If God's knowledge of Elias's choice depends on that contingent act, then God's knowledge is not identical to His necessary essence. Since God is absolutely simple, His knowledge is His being. If His knowledge is grounded in a creature's choice, then God's very existence becomes contingent upon the world, violating divine simplicity and aseity.

## Open questions
- Can a truth-conditional dependence of divine knowledge on creaturely acts be consistently classified as merely extrinsic if that knowledge is identical to the divine essence?
- Does the eternalist thesis that all time is present to God suffice to block the inference from the contingency of the object known to any contingency in the divine essence?
- Is a revised Grounding Principle that includes an explicit extrinsicness clause still compatible with the original motivation for linking divine foreknowledge to libertarian free will?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the Grounding Principle can be reformulated with an explicit extrinsicness clause so that the truth of divine knowledge depends on creaturely free choices without compromising divine simplicity or aseity. The case would develop a metaphysics of eternalist divine cognition on which the truth-conditions of God's knowledge are fixed by temporal objects through a real relation of reason that leaves the divine essence intrinsically unchanged, drawing on the distinction between the content of knowledge and the ontological ground of the knower. The strongest reply to anticipate is that if God's knowledge is identical to His essence, any truth-conditional dependence on contingent acts necessarily renders the essence itself contingent, a challenge the paper must meet by showing that extrinsic relationality to contingent objects does not introduce contingency into the essence of a simple being. To succeed, the paper would need to show not merely that this distinction is coherent, but that it is already implicit in the eternalist framework and can be stated without collapsing into the very modal dependence the objector identifies.

## Revised after the quality check
*The revision narrows the thesis from a general reformulation of the Grounding Principle to a claim about the eternalist, libertarian case, where the grounding must be stated as truth-dependence through an explicit extrinsicness clause, and it engages the existing extrinsic-model literature directly.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** On an eternalist, libertarian reading of the Grounding Principle, can the truth-conditions of divine foreknowledge depend on creaturely free choices without making the divine essence contingent? Does eternalism, rather than the simplicity problem as such, change how that dependence must be stated?

A paper here would argue that, for the eternalist and libertarian Grounding Principle, the dependence of divine knowledge on a free creaturely act is a dependence of truth-value on the act, not of the divine essence on the act. The paper engages the existing extrinsic-model literature directly, including Grant's treatment of contingent truths under divine simplicity, rather than treating it as background. Its specific contribution concerns the eternalist, libertarian case. Grounding by God's own contingent creative will is an intra-divine dependence on a will that God freely exercises, so the essence is not made to depend on anything outside itself. Grounding by free creaturely acts is different. Those acts must be open under the Anselmian thesis, so the grounding relation cannot be read as causal or sequential. On an eternal view, God's knowing is not located before the act, so no temporal or causal dependence of the knower is available. The paper argues that the grounding relation must therefore be restricted to the propositional content of divine knowledge, and it states this with an extrinsicness clause. The clause specifies that the relation holds between a proposition about God's knowing and the creaturely act, and that it does not hold between the act and the divine knowing relation or essence. Conditional necessities of the form 'if X then X' are used to show that this truth-conditional dependence does not make the knowing relation contingent. The paper treats the generic simplicity worry, which also arises for knowledge of what God creates, as a distinct and prior problem. It addresses that worry through the extrinsic model and does not claim the eternalist framing solves it. The paper's claim is limited: eternalism and the libertarian reading of the Grounding Principle require a specific extrinsicness clause, and that clause is what allows foreknowledge to be reconciled with open choices, which bears on the reconciliation dispute with Hasker's objections.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The paper concedes that the simplicity problem for contingent knowledge is generic and was already treated in the extrinsic-model literature, so it does not claim novelty for the simplicity point. Its distinct claim is narrower. The paper separates two grounding sources. Grounding by God's contingent creative will is intra-divine. Grounding by free creaturely acts is not, and under the Anselmian thesis those acts are open, so the dependence must be stated as a dependence of truth-value on the act. The extrinsicness clause makes that statement: the relation holds between the act and the propositional truth of divine knowledge, not between the act and the knowing relation or essence. The paper then uses the internal-necessity principle ('if X then X') to show that the resulting conditional structure introduces no contingency into the essence. This is an argument specific to the eternalist, libertarian Grounding Principle, and it engages the reconciliation dispute rather than restating the Thomist view.

*A competent reader would see this as the standard extrinsic-model answer to simplicity, relabelled as eternalist and libertarian without showing that those features matter, so it needs a real distinctive argument before it is a paper.*

- Strongest remaining objection: The extrinsicness clause is the familiar extrinsic or Cambridge-property move from the divine-simplicity literature (Grant, Brower, Stump–Kretzmann), and the eternalist, libertarian framing does no independent work. Whether 'God knows that Elias drinks' can vary across worlds while the essence stays fixed depends only on whether divine knowing is extrinsic; that holds or fails regardless of eternalism or the Anselmian openness of the act. Two of the paper's moves also fail. Under simplicity, God's will is God's essence, so dependence on 'God's contingent creative will' is not a safe intra-divine contrast case; it is the same problem. And 'if X then X' necessities concern whether the act is necessitated given the knowledge, not whether the essence varies across worlds, so they cannot show that no contingency enters the essence. The paper's claim to novelty therefore collapses into a restatement of the generic extrinsic model, with an asymmetry that does not hold.
- What it still needs: The paper must show a concrete case where an eternalist, libertarian Grounding Principle needs a different extrinsicness condition than the standard extrinsic model already gives for God's free creative choice, and why the asymmetry survives the identity of will and essence under simplicity. It should drop the misused 'if X then X' argument, engage Rogers's own defence of simplicity alongside Grant, Brower and Mullins, and answer the charge that purely extrinsic divine knowledge leaves God's knowing as a Cambridge property rather than knowledge.
