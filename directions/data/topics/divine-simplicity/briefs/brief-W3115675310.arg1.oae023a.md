## Challenged premise
**W3115675310.c005**: The claim that all attributes are identical is hard to accept because divine attributes like omnipotence, omniscience, and omnibenevolence are multiple.

## Objection
*reasoner:explorer · anthropic:sonnet*

Consider a lab with one sealed, perfectly calibrated instrument, the Gauge G, whose single dial reads a single value. Ask: how many 'properties' does G have? Its reading, its calibration, and its sensitivity to pressure all seem distinct, yet each is just the same physical state of one mechanism, described under different counterfactual and relational aspects. Now transpose: suppose a simple being S whose essence is pure act. Omnipotence is 'S can bring about any possible effect'; omniscience is 'S's knowledge is adequate to all truths'; omnibenevolence is 'S's will is directed to the good'. Each description picks out S under a different range of possible outcomes or relations to creatures. The plurality in c005 comes from the grammar of predication and from our finite concepts, not from any difference in what S is. So the premise's inference, from 'these attributes are multiple in our description' to 'they are multiple' and hence 'not identical', fails: the multiplicity is epistemic and conceptual, not ontological. The claim that all attributes are identical is therefore not shown hard to accept by the mere plurality of attribute terms.

## Open questions
- Can a difference in formal object or causal profile between divine attributes be grounded in one simple essence without entailing ontological composition?
- Does the revised premise requiring ontologically real multiplicity beg the question against divine simplicity by assuming that conceptual distinctness must map onto distinct properties?
- Is the Gauge G analogy undermined by disanalogies between physical instruments and a being of pure act, or does it successfully generalize to any simple ground of multiple descriptions?
- Can the defender show that the formally distinct intentional objects of omniscience and omnipotence are differences in what God is rather than differences in how one essence relates to creatures?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the common objection from attribute multiplicity against divine simplicity rests on an equivocation between conceptual plurality and ontological plurality. It would develop the Gauge G analogy to show that one simple state can ground many distinct descriptions, and it would examine whether defenders can supply a non-question-begging criterion for ontological multiplicity that does not already assume the falsity of simplicity. The strongest reply to anticipate is that divine attributes have formally distinct intentional objects and causal profiles, so their difference exceeds mere conceptual variation; the paper would need to show either that such formal distinctness is still compatible with a single divine act or that the defender's move to ontological multiplicity presupposes rather than establishes the incoherence of simplicity.

## Revised after the quality check
*The direction now concedes that the Gauge analogy fails, drops the claim that plural terms alone show merely conceptual plurality, and makes the modal discernibility argument the central target, answered by an intrinsic/relational distinction in predication.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** Does the modal discernibility of omniscience and omnipotence establish that divine attributes are ontologically distinct properties? If so, can a defender of divine simplicity block that inference by an account of predication on which an attribute's modal profile reflects the relations of one unchanging act to possible creatures and outcomes, rather than differences in what the act is?

A paper here would argue that the inference from attribute multiplicity to ontological multiplicity, as stated in c005, fails when it proceeds from plural attribute terms, but that a stronger argument from modal discernibility remains and can be answered only by an account of predication that separates intrinsic from relational modal profile. The paper will (1) concede that the Gauge analogy, as first formulated, does not work, because its components are modally separable states of one mechanism rather than one state under several descriptions; (2) replace it with a dispositional case in which one categorical base grounds counterfactual dispositions whose manifestations differ across possible inputs, so that modal divergence is carried by relations to inputs and not by any difference in the base; (3) apply this to the divine case by arguing that omnipotence, omniscience and omnibenevolence are specified by the range of outcomes and creaturely relations under which one unchanging act is manifested; and (4) state a criterion for when modal difference signals intrinsic difference: a difference counts as intrinsic only if it holds across variation in the relata, and it is shown that the discernibility argument needs the further premise that possible instances are fixed by the property alone. The paper then argues that the defender can reject that premise on grounds independent of the simplicity thesis, so the reply is not question-begging. The thesis is narrowed: it claims that simplicity survives the modal discernibility argument only if the divine attributes are relational profiles of a single act, and it does not claim that the plurality of attribute terms by itself shows that the attributes are not distinct.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The paper separates two sources of modal difference: differences in what a property is (intrinsic) and differences in which possible relata or outcomes one property is related to (relational). It rejects the referee's step from different possible instances to distinct properties. That step needs the premise that each possible instance is fixed by the property itself, not by the property together with variable creaturely conditions. The repaired dispositional case shows that one categorical base can have modally divergent instances with no difference in the base, so the premise is not analytic. The paper then applies this to omnipotence, omniscience and omnibenevolence, read as relational profiles of an unchanging act, and argues that the relational reading is supported by the grounds of the modal claims. The Gauge analogy is dropped as written.

**Re-assessment** (fresh read, the assessor did not see its earlier critique): **not yet defensible** · overall 2.75/5 · coherence 3/5 · robustness 2/5 · significance 2/5 · specificity 4/5

*This is a clearly organized but largely familiar defense of simplicity against Plantinga that founders on attributes whose objects are not creaturely, so it is not publishable until it confronts that problem and the existing Stump–Kretzmann, Mann and Brower literature.*

- Strongest remaining objection: The relational-profile strategy only works if every modal difference between the attributes varies with creaturely relata. It does not. Omniscience includes God's knowledge of necessary truths and of the divine essence itself, which holds fixed across all variation in creatures, including worlds with no creation. In those same worlds omnipotence has no manifestation. So there is a modal difference that survives the paper's own criterion in step (4), and it is intrinsic by that criterion. The dispositional analogy also faces a problem. On the leading accounts of powers (Martin, Heil's powerful qualities, Bird's dispositional essentialism), a categorical base with distinct manifestation-types is either internally differentiated or individuated by distinct stimulus-manifestation pairs. The model therefore imports the composition it was meant to remove. Making the attributes relational also risks rendering divine knowledge and goodness merely extrinsic (Cambridge) predicates. And the contingency of divine willing, an intrinsic difference across worlds in what the one act is, is left untouched.
- What it still needs: The paper must say how the relational reading handles attributes whose objects are not creaturely, such as God's self-knowledge, knowledge of necessary truths, and love of the divine good. It must defend a theory of powers on which one base grounds distinct manifestation-types without internal distinction. It must also engage the existing literature that already runs this line: Stump–Kretzmann's analogy of the sun's single power, Mann's property-instance view, Brower's truthmaker account, and Pruss on simplicity and contingency.
