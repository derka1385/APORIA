## Challenged premise

## Objection
*reasoner:explorer · glm:zai-org/GLM-5.2*

Two trained Thomistic theologians, A and B, both accept the analogical framework and its fixed metaphysical relation between creatures and God. A argues 'God is mutable' is inadmissible: mutability belongs to creatures as material composites, and the metaphysical relation traces only perfections to God. B argues 'God is mutable' is admissible: creaturely mutability includes responsiveness to love, a perfection rooted in divine goodness, so the analogical relation permits the predication when understood analogically. Both invoke the same fixed metaphysical relation to creatures. The framework provides no principled mechanism to settle whether mutability traces to material potency (excluded) or to responsive goodness (included). Thus the fixed metaphysical relation fails to supply constraints sufficient to exclude 'God is mutable'—a predication classical theism must reject. The framework's exclusion power depends on prior, independent judgments about which creaturely features count as perfections—judgments the framework itself does not generate.

## Strongest responses and why they failed
- **Defender A**: The framework's constraint is not a decision procedure; it requires that a predicate trace to a pure perfection, and it is the metaphysics of creaturely perfection, not analogical semantics, that decides which features count. The disagreement over 'mutable' therefore shows only that the framework is incomplete at the margins, not that it lacks exclusion power. — *The defender conceded that the framework's exclusion power depends on prior, independent judgments about which creaturely features are pure perfections, and that the original claim does not survive without qualification; the revised premise makes admissibility conditional on a prior classification of pure perfections.*
- **Defender B**: The fixed metaphysical relation is embedded in the metaphysics of participation, so that only pure perfections involving no limitation or potency are admissible, and mutability as such implies potency and change, which the metaphysics of pure act excludes; theologian B confuses a creaturely mode with a pure perfection. — *The defender conceded that the prior identification of which creaturely features are pure perfections is delivered by natural theology rather than by the analogical framework itself, so the argument survives only by acknowledging a division of labor that leaves the framework dependent on external natural-theological judgments.*

## Open questions
- Does making admissibility constraints conditional on prior natural-theological judgments about pure perfections undermine the claim that analogy itself supplies sufficiently determinate content for divine predicates?
- Can the res significata and modus significandi distinction be reformulated so that contested cases such as 'God is mutable' are settled by semantic structure rather than by prior metaphysical classifications?
- If the analogical framework is incomplete at the margins, how wide is the range of contested divine predications, and does this incompleteness affect predicates central to religious practice?
- Does the dependence of analogical semantics on natural theology constitute a fatal circularity in the defense of divine simplicity, or is it consistent with standard Thomistic methodology?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the Thomistic analogical framework lacks independent exclusion power over ill-formed divine predications because its admissibility constraints presuppose prior natural-theological judgments about which creaturely features count as pure perfections. The case would center on contested predications such as 'God is mutable,' showing that the framework's fixed metaphysical relation and the distinction between res significata and modus significandi underdetermine verdicts when theologians disagree over whether a feature is a perfection or a defect. The strongest reply to anticipate is that this merely reflects the standard Thomistic division of labor: analogy governs the mode of predication, while natural theology supplies the content by identifying pure perfections through the metaphysics of participation and pure act. To succeed, the paper would need to show either that this division of labor renders analogy insufficient for the determinacy-of-meaning task the original argument assigned to it, or that the natural-theological classification of perfections itself relies on prior semantic or metaphysical commitments that are equally contested, so that the framework's constraints are not independently principled.
