## Challenged premise

## Objection
*tradition_lens · gemma:google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it*

A Supreme Being is both perfectly Just and perfectly Wise. In Platonism, Justice and Wisdom are distinct, eternal, and independent universals. The Being instantiates both, making it a composite of these real, independent properties. However, the Being does not depend on Justice or Wisdom as final causes to realize its nature or achieve its existence. The Being is the locus of these properties, but the properties exist independently of the Being. The existence of a plurality of real, instantiated properties does not imply that the being is directed toward those properties for its own completion or that they act as its final cause. A being can be many-featured through the instantiation of independent universals without being causally dependent upon them. W4229080938.c012 fails because the instantiation of independent, real properties does not necessitate that those properties function as final causes or sources of causal dependence.

## Open questions
- Does the relation of instantiation between a necessary being and independent Platonic universals constitute an existential dependence that violates aseity, or is it merely a non-dependent characterization?
- If a being's unity is nothing over and above its exemplification of distinct properties, is that being a composite in the sense required by divine simplicity?
- Can the argument for divine simplicity survive the shift from causal dependence to grounding dependence without becoming a distinct argument with different metaphysical commitments?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the traditional simplicity argument against a composite God must abandon its reliance on final-causal dependence and instead be reformulated in terms of grounding: if a necessary being's unity consists in nothing over and above its exemplification of distinct Platonic universals, then its existence is asymmetrically grounded in constituents external to itself, violating aseity. The case would develop a metaphysics of instantiation showing that being the single subject of independent forms still leaves the being derivative for its character and unity, using the concession that final causation is not the only route to dependence. The strongest reply to anticipate is that instantiation is merely a non-dependent relation of exemplification that characterizes the being without constituting it. The paper would need to show that this 'single subject' account cannot explain unity without covertly presupposing a prior whole that is itself grounded in the forms, and that such grounding dependence is incompatible with existing through oneself alone.

## Revised after the quality check
*The revision drops final-causal dependence as the operative route, replaces it with a characterization-versus-constitution distinction, and narrows the thesis to a conditional result that holds only for bundle-type unity.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** Does a composite, Platonist God face an aseity threat that the simplicity argument uniquely identifies, or is the threat fully explained by the independent existence of the forms, which would leave simplicity idle?

A paper here would argue that the simplicity argument against a composite God does not establish its conclusion through final-causal dependence, and that the defensible residue is narrower than the original claim. Premise c012 fails as stated: instantiating independent, real properties does not by itself make those properties final causes or sources of causal dependence, so the paper drops final causation as the operative route. It then asks whether any non-final route survives. The paper distinguishes two claims a Platonist can accept: that exemplification characterizes a subject, and that exemplification constitutes the subject's unity or existence. It argues that on a bundle-type view of unity the second claim holds, so composition generates grounding of the subject's existence in constituents other than itself, and simplicity is needed to block that dependence. On a substratum or primitive-substance view the subject is prior to its instantiations, so only the first claim holds. There, composition yields no grounding of existence, and the aseity pressure comes from the independent existence of the forms, which threatens God whether or not God is composite. The paper therefore defends a conditional result: simplicity's force is confined to bundle-type unity, and for substratum theists the threat is a separate aseity problem about the forms that the simplicity argument cannot address. The paper does not claim that the grounding recasting of the argument is new. Its contribution is the conditional result and a test for which case a given theist occupies.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The referee's novelty objection is answered by narrowing: the paper does not present the grounding recasting as new, and it concedes that the bundle-based argument applies only to bundle-type unity. The bundle-ontology charge is answered by making the result explicitly conditional, so the paper does not assume that the Platonic theist is a bundle theorist. For substratum views, the paper accepts the referee's point that the forms threaten aseity regardless of composition, and it treats this as a limit on simplicity's role rather than a refutation of the simplicity argument in general. The distinction used throughout is between exemplification as characterization of a subject and exemplification as constitution of the subject's unity or existence. The paper's claim is that only the second generates grounding dependence, and that whether a given theist is committed to the second is a question the paper settles by the test it proposes.

**Re-assessment** (fresh read, the assessor did not see its earlier critique): **not yet defensible** · overall 2.75/5 · coherence 3/5 · robustness 2/5 · significance 2/5 · specificity 4/5

*A competent but largely derivative project: the core conditional is Wolterstorff's point in new dress, and the bundle/substratum framing misdescribes the relevant views, so it needs a rebuilt taxonomy and an actual test before it is a paper.*

- Strongest remaining objection: The conditional result is already in the literature, and its taxonomy is wrong. Wolterstorff's 'Divine Simplicity' (1991) showed that simplicity arguments get their grip only within a constituent ontology. On a relational ontology like Plantinga's, God's having distinct properties is not composition, and the threat becomes a separate aseity problem about abstracta (Plantinga 1980; Bergmann and Brower 2006). The paper's 'substratum' case also conflates two different views. A relational Platonist view has no constituents. A substratum view does have constituents: the substratum plus its attributes make up a thick particular that is plausibly grounded in them. So the claim that substratum composition 'yields no grounding of existence' is false for the constituent version and redundant for the relational one. The view the Avicennan/Ghazālian argument actually targets, a hylomorphic or essence-existence constituent ontology, is left out entirely.
- What it still needs: It must engage Wolterstorff and the constituent/relational literature directly. It must replace the bundle/substratum dichotomy with a taxonomy that separates constituent from relational ontologies and includes the essence-existence model Ghazālī actually addresses. And it must say concretely what its promised 'test' is, then show it delivers something beyond the existing diagnosis.
