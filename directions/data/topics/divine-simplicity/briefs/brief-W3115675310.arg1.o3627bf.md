## Challenged premise
**W3115675310.c006**: Divine simplicity also implies that God is not a personal creator but an abstract object, since properties are abstract objects.

## Objection
*reasoner:skeptic · glm:zai-org/GLM-5.2*

Consider trope theory, where properties are particularized, concrete entities rather than abstract universals. The redness of a specific apple is a concrete particular, not an abstract object. Now suppose divine simplicity identifies God with the divine essence, and the divine essence is a trope—a concrete, particularized property rather than a universal. On this view, God is identical to a concrete particular property, not an abstract object. God remains concrete, causally active, and capable of intentional action. A trope can be instantiated once and bear causal relations, so there is no entailment from 'God is identical to a property' to 'God is an abstract object.' The premise only follows if one assumes a Platonic universal theory of properties, where all properties are abstract, but this is not the only available metaphysics of properties. The move from 'God is a property' to 'God is abstract' requires an unstated and contestable assumption about the nature of properties.

## Open questions
- Can a single concrete trope bear the causal and intentional properties required for personal theism, or does identifying God with one property-particular inevitably collapse personhood into propertyhood?
- Does the revised conditional premise merely relocate the personal-creator objection, or does trope theory provide independent metaphysical resources for showing that a property-particular can be a subject of knowledge and will?
- If the divine essence is a trope, how is it metaphysically distinct from ordinary concrete tropes, and can it avoid being treated as a sui generis entity that strains standard trope theory?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that trope theory undermines the Plantinga-style inference from divine simplicity to God's abstractness by showing that the premise 'properties are abstract objects' encodes a contestable Platonic assumption rather than a necessary truth. The case would develop a trope-theoretic model of the divine essence as a concrete, particularized property that remains causally active, thereby blocking the step from 'God is identical to a property' to 'God is an abstract object.' The strongest reply to anticipate is that even on trope theory, identifying God with a single property—whether abstract universal or concrete trope—makes God a property rather than a person, so the personal-creator worry persists in a new form. To succeed, the paper would need to show either that a single trope can possess the complexity required for personal agency, knowledge, and will, or that the residual worry is no more damaging to divine simplicity than to alternative metaphysical accounts of God.

A paper here would argue that premise c006 is invalid as stated: the step from 'God is identical to a property' to 'God is an abstract object' needs the Platonic premise that all properties are abstract, and a trope-theoretic reading of the divine essence blocks that step. The paper then narrows its claim. It does not argue that a bearerless trope is thereby a personal agent. It argues that once the abstractness charge is removed, the personhood objection becomes a parity question: it bears on divine simplicity only if simplicity supplies no resource for subjecthood that a non-simple theism lacks. The paper states what a subject of knowledge and will would have to be on the trope view, and tests whether non-simple theism, where God is a bearer of many properties, secures a subject by another route. If it does not, the personhood worry is shared by both views. If it does, the paper identifies that as a genuine cost of simplicity rather than dissolving it.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The referee is right that blocking c006 is the standard move and that Mann's property-instance view already occupies it, so the paper claims no novelty for that step. It adds an explicit distinction between being a property, an ontological category, and being a subject, a bearer of knowledge and will, and it concedes that the trope view does not by itself establish the second. The sense of 'concrete' is fixed as particularity and causal efficacy rather than non-abstractness, since trope theorists themselves call tropes abstract particulars. The personhood worry is then tested by parity against rival non-simple accounts, the alternative the referee offered, rather than deferred as a disjunction. Only if that test favours simplicity does the paper claim more than a partial result.

*The paper is clearly organised but mostly re-treads Mann's reply, and its new parity test looks likely to confirm the very cost it hopes to neutralise, so it needs a substantive account of a self-subsisting trope as subject before it is worth writing.*

- Strongest remaining objection: The trope move is not new: Mann's property-instance reply to Plantinga already makes it, and the paper concedes this. The new part, the parity test, looks like a foregone conclusion. Non-simple theism secures a subject by the most ordinary route: God is a substance that bears knowledge and will as properties. The 'bearerless particular as subject' problem arises only because simplicity removes the bearer. So the test almost certainly comes out against simplicity, and the paper ends where Plantinga began. Plantinga's real worry was always that a property, abstract or not, cannot know or will, and abstractness was only an aggravating feature.
- What it still needs: The paper needs a positive account of how a self-subsisting trope fills the substance-and-subject role. One route is Campbell-style tropes capable of independent existence, combined with the Thomist notion of ipsum esse subsistens. Another is to engage Brower's truthmaker reading, which denies that simplicity makes God 'a property' at all. Without one of these, the parity test must be shown to be non-trivial, for example by targeting bundle-theoretic or other specific non-simple accounts that also lack a robust bearer.
