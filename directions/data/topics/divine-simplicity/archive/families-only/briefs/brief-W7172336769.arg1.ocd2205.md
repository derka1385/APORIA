## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · kimi:moonshotai/Kimi-K2.6*

A parish council must decide whether to shelter refugees. They ask their pastor whether God commands hospitality. Under the Thomistic analogical framework, 'hospitable' is deemed admissible and said primarily of God, while 'cruel' is excluded. Yet the framework assigns no truth-conditional sense to 'God is hospitable': it does not specify whether divine hospitality welcomes all strangers unconditionally or only the righteous. Because the council cannot derive any actionable content from the predicate beyond its bare label, they remain unable to decide whether turning families away violates God's nature. The formal constraint on admissible words therefore fails to guide practice; without at least partial truth-conditional content, the distinction between primary and derivative predication collapses into a mere lexical permission list. The promised determinacy for practice is illusory.

## Open questions
- Can a purely negative constraint on admissible predications still count as making theological language 'sufficiently determinate for practice,' or does practical guidance require partial positive truth-conditions?
- If the doctrine of analogy must be supplemented by negation and eminence to yield actionable content, does the resulting view remain genuinely analogical or does it collapse into a qualified univocity?
- Does the parish-council test set an unfairly high standard for semantic determinacy, given that ordinary moral language also leaves hard cases underdetermined?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the Thomistic doctrine of analogy, understood as supplying only principled constraints on admissible predicates, fails to make theological language sufficiently determinate for contested practical decisions, and that attempts to strengthen it by appealing to partial truth-conditional content via negation and eminence either collapse analogy into a qualified univocity or still leave hard cases undetermined. The case would turn on the parish-council scenario, showing that knowing 'hospitable' applies primarily and 'cruel' is excluded does not determine whether divine hospitality is unconditional, and that narrowing the claim to 'clear cases' trivializes the doctrine's practical relevance. The strongest reply to anticipate is that the semantic-metaphysical framework of res significata and modus significandi supplies enough partial positive content to guide core decisions without full specification; the paper would need to show that this partial content either imports univocal sense illicitly or is too thin to settle the borderline cases that actually trouble religious practice.

The test case uses a pure perfection, not a mixed one. Take 'good'. For a human agent, 'a good person who can prevent a child's serious suffering at no cost to anyone else prevents it' carries a determinate creaturely verdict, because the ordinary sense of 'good' is fixed by a shared standard of practical reasoning about harm and benefit. Applied to God, the framework supplies the res significata of goodness, and the modus significandi that ties the creaturely verdict to human practical reasoning does not transfer. The paper would show that the analogical rules of primary and derivative predication fix that God is good, but supply no further rule settling whether divine goodness requires the same verdict in this case.
