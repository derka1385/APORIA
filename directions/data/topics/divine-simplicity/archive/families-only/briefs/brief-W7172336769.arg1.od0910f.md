## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · qwen:Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B*

A parish priest, Father Maren, must preach on 'God is one.' He applies the res/modus distinction: the modus significandi ('X is one') is identical in 'God is one' and 'a rowing team is one'; the res significata differs (divine oneness vs. team unity). He now asks what divine oneness consists in—simplicity, absence of composition, unity of attributes? The distinction tells him only that the res differs from team unity. It labels a gap. It offers no criterion for filling the res significata slot with determinate content. Maren cannot move from 'God is one, differently from a team is one' to any positive account of what divine oneness is. The modus is fixed; the res remains a placeholder the framework itself cannot populate. Without a fill, no predicate is grounded; only its indeterminacy is named. The distinction sorts predicates into 'divine' and 'human' slots but supplies nothing that makes the divine slot meaningful rather than merely labeled.

## Open questions

- Can the fixed referent of 'God' supply determinate content to the res significata independently of a complete theory of analogy, or does it presuppose the very determinacy that is in question?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the Thomistic doctrine of analogy cannot discharge the determinacy requirement for meaningful theological predication if the res significata is populated solely by the via negativa and eminence, because exclusion and relative attribution produce only a negatively constrained placeholder rather than positive content adequate for a speaker to know what is being affirmed. The case would develop the 'Maren' scenario to show that sorting predicates into divine and creaturely slots does not by itself yield determinate meaning, and would scrutinize whether the broader analogical framework—including the fixed referent of 'God' and principled constraints on predication—can supply the missing positive content. The strongest reply to anticipate is that Aquinas's own practice treats negation and eminence as yielding sufficient partial content for preaching and worship, and that meaning does not require complete specification. To succeed, the paper would need to show that this reply equivocates between two standards of determinacy: excluding candidate contents versus providing a positively specifiable account of the divine attribute, and to establish that the weaker standard is insufficient for the argument's conclusion that theological language is determinate enough for meaning and practice.

The res is the perfection itself, known from creatures and affirmed of God per prius, so the distinction does not leave it as a placeholder. The live question is whether that perfection, once every creaturely mode is removed, still discriminates one candidate content from another. The paper narrows its thesis to this residue. It argues that meaning requires a discriminating standard: a predicate must fix which perfection is affirmed and which contrary it excludes, so that a speaker can grasp what is affirmed without grasping what it is (the that-it-is versus what-it-is distinction). It tests this standard on 'God is good', where the contrary is creaturely evil and the perfection is determinate through its contrary, and on 'God is one', where, following ST I.11.1, negation is the content. The paper claims the second case is where the gap bites: exclusion fixes the content of 'one' only if the excluded contrary (division) is itself determinate. The paper argues that the analogical framework, with its fixed referent and principled constraints on predication, supplies enough to make the contrary determinate in the first case and in the second only partly, and it leaves the residual indeterminacy of the second case explicit rather than resolved.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The paper accepts the referee's correction. The res is the perfection and the modus is the creaturely mode, so the Maren framing is dropped. *The corrected framing is an improvement, but the chosen standard and test cases misread Aquinas on transcendental unity and privation, so the paper as planned would be dismissed by a competent Thomist reader unless it is rebuilt around positive perfection terms.*

- Strongest remaining objection: The proposed test cases misfire against Aquinas's own view. 'One' is the transcendental unum, which by ST I.11.1 adds to being only the negation of division. Its content is therefore being plus a fully determinate negation (being undivided in itself). Finding 'God is one' only 'partly' determinate is either no news or a misreading. Making 'good' determinate 'through its contrary' inverts Aquinas's order of explanation: evil is a privation of good, so good is prior. That order is also why ST I.13.2 rejects Maimonides' purely negative or causal reading of perfection terms. The paper's discriminating-contrary standard is thus an apophatic standard that Thomists explicitly refuse. And the 'candidates' it says must be discriminated (simplicity, absence of composition, unity of attributes) coincide under divine simplicity, so there is no indeterminacy to resolve.
- What it still needs: It must replace the contrary-exclusion standard with one that engages Aquinas's claim that perfection terms signify the divine substance positively, though imperfectly (ST I.13.2–5). It must then show, for a pure perfection such as 'wise' or 'good' rather than the transcendental 'one', that the res abstracted from every creaturely mode fails to discriminate between rival contents, engaging the actual analogy literature (e.g., Burrell, Te Velde, Rocca, Ross).
