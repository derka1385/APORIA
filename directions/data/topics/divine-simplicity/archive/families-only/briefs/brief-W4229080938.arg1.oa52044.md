## Challenged premise
**W4229080938.c012**: If God were not simple, he would depend on properties like power, wisdom, and blessedness as final causes.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · kimi:moonshotai/Kimi-K2.6*

Consider the necessary being called the Foundation. The Foundation is essentially composed of three irreducible perfections: potency, knowledge, and bliss. These are really distinct features, so the Foundation is not simple. Nevertheless, the Foundation exists through itself; its existence is absolutely necessary and not teleologically ordered toward these perfections. The three perfections are not final causes—they are not ends for the sake of which the Foundation exists—but are co-eternal constituents or necessary formal aspects of its nature. The Foundation does not depend on them as goals to be realized; rather, it simply has them necessarily as part of what it is. Therefore, a being can be composite with distinct properties without depending on those properties as final causes.

## Open questions
- Is constitutive dependence on distinct necessary properties genuinely incompatible with aseity, or can a being exist through itself while having really distinct metaphysical constituents?
- Does the revised argument against composition covertly beg the question by defining aseity in a way that presupposes the impossibility of distinct metaphysical parts?
- Can the traditional distinction between abstract perfections as formal aspects of a simple being and as distinct entities be defended without already assuming divine simplicity?
- What principled criterion distinguishes a composite necessary being with irreducible perfections from a simple being signified under different formal aspects?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the inference from divine aseity to absolute simplicity cannot be repaired simply by widening the dependence premise to include constitutive or grounding dependence, because the objector's model of a necessarily self-existent yet metaphysically composite being demonstrates that really distinct constituents need not function as causes—efficient, final, or grounding—of the whole's existence. The case would develop the Foundation thought experiment to distinguish between constituents that ground a nature and causes on which a being depends, and would press the question of whether existing through oneself is compatible with having irreducible metaphysical parts. The strongest reply to anticipate is the defender's claim that any real distinction among perfections amounts to a form of grounding dependence that compromises aseity, effectively treating composition as sufficient for ontological dependence. To succeed, the paper would need to show either that the concept of a composite yet absolutely independent necessary being is internally incoherent, or that the defender's widened dependence premise begs the question by defining aseity in a way that excludes complexity rather than demonstrating its incompatibility.

## Revised after the quality check
*The direction now narrows the thesis to a conditional claim about whole-priority and parts-priority, replaces the stipulated Foundation with a test of the referee's unifier and parts-posterior principles, and drops the self-undermining incoherence success condition.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** If the dependence premise of the aseity-to-simplicity argument is widened to cover grounding dependence on distinct constituents, does that rule out a necessary being that exists through itself yet has really distinct parts, once we settle whether a whole is prior to or posterior to its parts?

A paper here would argue that the aseity-to-simplicity inference does not go through merely by widening the dependence premise from final causation to constitutive or grounding dependence. The argument would be narrowed to a conditional claim. Whether a composite necessary being is ruled out depends on a further thesis about the priority relation between a whole and its proper parts. Under parts-priority, where the whole is grounded in its parts, a composite Foundation would depend on its perfections and aseity would be violated, so the widened premise does its work. Under whole-priority, where the whole is prior and the parts depend on the whole, the Foundation's constituents do not ground its existence, and the widened premise is not triggered. The paper would not rely on the Foundation as a stipulated counterexample. Instead it would take the two principles the referee presses, that composition requires a ground of composition and that a whole is posterior to its proper parts, and test each. For the unifier principle, the paper would ask whether the ground of composition must be distinct from the whole. If the unifying ground can be the being itself, then the principle yields no dependence on anything other than the whole, and the defender needs an extra premise to get dependence on something distinct. The paper would argue that this extra premise is what the aseity argument requires, and that it is not supplied by aseity alone. It would also drop the success condition of proving composite necessary beings incoherent, since that would vindicate the defender. The contribution would be a precise statement of what the widened premise needs in order to yield simplicity, with a whole-priority model of essential parthood as the test case, and an engagement with the existing literature on aseity and divine complexity.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The referee is right that the Foundation case, taken alone, stipulates that its perfections are non-grounding constituents, so the paper does not rest on it. The reply has three parts. First, the paper accepts that the final-cause version of c012 is too narrow and concedes that point. Second, it does not assume that composition yields no dependence; it asks which priority relation holds between the whole and its parts, and it shows that the answer decides whether widened dependence applies. Third, it answers the unifier principle directly: a ground of composition establishes dependence on something distinct only if that ground is not the whole itself. If it is the whole, aseity is intact. The paper's claim is that aseity plus widened dependence does not entail simplicity without this further premise, and that the defender has to supply it.
