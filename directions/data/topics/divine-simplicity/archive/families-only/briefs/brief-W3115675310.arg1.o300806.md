## Challenged premise
**W3115675310.c003**: If divine simplicity is true, then every divine attribute is identical to the divine essence, making all attributes identical with each other.

## Objection
*tradition_lens · gemma:google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it*

A saint participates in the uncreated light of God. This light is not the unknowable essence of God, yet it is truly God. God is simple in His essence, but He communicates Himself through distinct, uncreated energies. When we speak of His love, His justice, or His power, we are describing these divine energies. These energies are distinct from the essence, yet they are not separate from God. They provide multiplicity in how God acts and is known without compromising His essential unity. Divine attributes correspond to these energies rather than the essence itself, preserving both simplicity and the reality of distinct divine properties.

## Open questions
- Does the essence-energies distinction introduce a form of composition that is incompatible with any plausible account of divine simplicity?
- Can the classical Western identification of attributes with the divine essence be defended once the Palamite alternative is granted as internally consistent?
- Are divine attributes best understood as identical with the essence, grounded in distinct energies, or as conceptual distinctions without ontological separation?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the Palamite essence-energies distinction provides a coherent metaphysical framework that blocks the standard inference from divine simplicity to the identity of all divine attributes. The case would proceed by showing that if divine attributes are grounded in distinct uncreated energies rather than the unknowable essence, the collapse of all attributes into a single property does not follow, a point both defenders conceded requires narrowing the original premise. The strongest reply to anticipate is that any real distinction between essence and energies introduces composition into God and thereby abandons genuine simplicity. To succeed, the paper would need to show that the essence-energies distinction is not merely a verbal maneuver but a principled way to preserve divine unity while allowing real multiplicity in how God acts and is known, and it would need to defend this reading of simplicity against the charge that it is ad hoc or metaphysically costly.

**Revised question:** Can the Palamite essence-energies distinction be stated without composition in God, and does it show anything about Plantinga's conditional, or does it only deny the strong doctrine that the conditional targets?

A paper here would argue that the Palamite essence-energies distinction does not refute Plantinga's conditional, because it denies the strong doctrine of simplicity that the conditional targets, but that the denial is a principled and non-composite alternative which must be assessed on its own terms. The paper would first fix a non-question-begging statement of what simplicity requires on the Palamite view: God's essence has no parts, no accidents, and no distinction between a substance and properties that it has, and attributes are predicated of God through his uncreated energies, which are his own operations and self-manifestations rather than constituents of his essence. It would then argue that the essence-energies distinction is neither composition nor a relocation of the attributes outside God. Composition requires that the distinct items be among the things God is; the energies are what God does and how he is given to creatures, and the paper would give a criterion for distinguishing these two relations so that the charge of composition can be tested rather than assumed. The relocation charge fails if the energies are uncreated and are God's own activity, so that the attributes remain God's own properties in the sense relevant to predication. The paper would then say what it adds beyond the existing Bradshaw and Palamas-on-simplicity literature by testing this criterion against truthmaker-based and Brower-style defences of classical simplicity, asking whether those defences, which locate the truthmakers of divine predications in the one essence, can accommodate the energies without reintroducing the multiplicity that Plantinga's argument targets. The paper does not claim that the Palamite view is the only or the first way to resist the identity of attributes, and it does not claim that it refutes the conditional for classical simplicity.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The referee is right that the Palamite view denies the strong doctrine, so the paper concedes that it does not show c003 false as Plantinga states it. It replies on two points. First, it grants that the energies-theory is a rival doctrine and asks what that doctrine must say to be coherent. Second, it answers the composition charge by distinguishing real distinction among constituents of a being from distinction among a being's uncreated operations and modes of self-manifestation. The paper states the criterion explicitly and tests whether the energies meet it. The relocation charge is answered by showing that uncreated energies are God's own activity, not created effects, so the attributes stay God's own. Whether this distinction is stable is the paper's central test; if it is not, the paper should conclude that the Palamite view is a costly denial of simplicity rather than a coherent alternative.

- Strongest remaining objection: The proposed criterion separating 'what God is' from 'what God does' cannot carry the weight placed on it. The energies are said to be uncreated, eternal, 'truly God', and really (not merely conceptually) distinct from the essence. Classical simplicity, which treats God as actus purus, denies exactly such a real distinction between God's being and his eternal activity. So either the energies count among what God is, which gives real distinction within God and hence the composition or metaphysical complexity that simplicity forbids, or they do not, in which case the relocation charge returns: the attributes belong to something other than God's essence. The reply to relocation ('uncreated, therefore God's own') assumes what it needs to prove. And the 'operations versus constituents' line re-describes the essence-energies distinction instead of giving an independent test of it. A further problem is that eternal, necessary energies such as love and power behave like essential properties. If any of them are contingent, God has contingent uncreated features, which raises its own difficulties. The paper never says which.
- What it still needs: The paper must state a composition criterion that is motivated independently of Palamite theology, and show that it classifies uncreated, eternal, really distinct energies as non-constituents without also clearing other forms of divine complexity that simplicity is meant to exclude. It also needs a non-circular answer to relocation that engages directly with Mullins, Tollefsen, and Bradshaw.
