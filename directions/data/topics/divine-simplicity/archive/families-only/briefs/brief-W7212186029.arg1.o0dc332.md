## Challenged premise
**W7212186029.c002**: The two formulations are not generally equivalent because, on some plausible mereological theories, something can lack proper parts by being a part of all its parts.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · glm:zai-org/GLM-5.2*

Consider two distinct objects A and B, where A is part of B and B is part of A. The premise needs exactly such a case: A lacks proper parts (since A is part of every part of A—A is part of A and part of B), yet A is not identical to all its parts, since A ≠ B. This would show the two formulations of divine simplicity diverge. But if A is part of B, transitivity makes every part of A a part of B. If B is part of A, every part of B is a part of A. So A and B have exactly the same parts. Mereological extensionality—objects with the same parts are identical—is a core commitment of any plausible mereological theory. Any theory permitting distinct mutual parts must reject extensionality and is therefore not plausible. The 'plausible mereological theories' the premise requires do not exist.

## Revised after the quality check
*The revision drops the claim that extensionality is constitutive of parthood, shows that the objection presupposes antisymmetry, specifies an intermediate non-identity between God and his attributes, and narrows the conclusion to a conditional claim about whether the symmetry formulation counts as simplicity.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** Can a non-antisymmetric mereology supply a coherent, distinct formal thesis for the symmetry formulation of divine simplicity, on which God and his attributes are mutual parts with no proper parts and no multiplicity of constituents? And does that thesis count as a doctrine of simplicity, or only as a weaker, separate doctrine?

A paper here would argue that the symmetry formulation of divine simplicity is a coherent and distinct thesis within non-antisymmetric mereology, but that it counts as a doctrine of simplicity only on one specific account of the non-identity between God and his attributes. The paper first disposes of the objection that extensionality is constitutive of parthood. Given reflexivity and transitivity, the principle that objects with the same parts are identical entails antisymmetry, so the objection assumes the point at issue. The existential claim that some plausible non-antisymmetric theories exist therefore survives, and the paper defends it by engaging the existing non-antisymmetric mereology literature (Cotnoir; Cotnoir and Bacon) rather than by assertion. The paper also corrects the formal point: mutual parthood is symmetric parthood, so the symmetry formulation is not derived from non-symmetric parthood. Its central claim is that God and an attribute can be distinct objects standing in mutual parthood while sharing all their parts. This is not a distinction between constituents, since neither has a proper part the other lacks, so it avoids the first horn of the referee's dilemma. It is also not merely conceptual, because it is a structural fact about the mereological model and not a fact about how we conceive the terms, so it avoids the second horn. The paper specifies this intermediate account precisely and argues that the classical requirements of no composition, no real plurality of constituents, and no non-conceptual grounding of distinction are met by it. It then states the cost openly: if the classical doctrine requires strict identity of God with his attributes, the symmetry formulation is a weaker, distinct thesis that captures part of simplicity, not the whole. The paper also flags as an unstated premise that instantiation can be modelled as parthood, and it treats that premise as something requiring independent defence.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The paper answers the referee's dilemma by introducing a third kind of distinction. God and an attribute are non-identical as objects but share all their parts, so the distinction is structural rather than conceptual, and it does not yield a plurality of proper constituents, so it is not real composition. The paper answers the mereological worry by showing that 'same parts implies identity' is equivalent to antisymmetry given reflexivity and transitivity, so the objection begs the question, and by defending non-antisymmetric mereology on its own terms. Where the classical doctrine demands more than this, the paper concedes that the symmetry formulation is a distinct, weaker thesis.

*A competent formal setup with a correct answer to the extensionality objection, but its central theological move, a primitive 'structural' distinction between God and his attributes, looks like a real distinction under another name, and the paper is not publishable until it says what grounds that distinction.*

- Strongest remaining objection: The proposed 'third kind of distinction' collapses into a real distinction. If God and an attribute are numerically distinct objects that share all their parts, nothing in the mereology grounds their non-identity, so it is bare numerical distinctness. Bare numerical distinctness between God and goodness, and between goodness and power (which by transitivity are also mutual parts), is exactly the plurality of really distinct items in God that classical simplicity denies. Calling it 'structural rather than conceptual' relabels the problem and treats a feature of a formal model as if it were a metaphysical ground of distinction. On this reading the view introduces a plurality of distinct divine entities rather than avoiding composition, which is arguably worse for simplicity than having proper parts.
- What it still needs: The paper must say what makes God and an attribute numerically distinct despite sharing all their parts, and show that this ground does not count as a real distinction by the tradition's own standards (for example the Thomist distinctio rationis cum fundamento in re versus distinctio realis), while dealing with the multiplication of mutually-part attributes. It should also drop its mistaken 'correction' of c008 and supply an actual defence of modelling instantiation as parthood.
