## Challenged premise
**W7203485685.c008**: A third-personal proposition identifying God as actualising a world does not itself represent that actualisation under the first-personal aspect required for source attribution.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · openai:gpt-5.6-sol*

God’s cognition uses only a rigid identifier, “G,” for the unique being whose intentions directly control which world becomes actual. G considers W0 and W1, judges W1 better, forms the intention expressed as “G will actualise W1,” and W1 consequently becomes actual. G then knows the proposition “G actualised W1 rather than W0 because G intended W1.” The identifier is third-personal: it carries no first-personal mode of presentation. Yet the proposition identifies the source perfectly. It singles out the unique controller, connects that being’s intention to the resulting world, and excludes every alternative source. Nothing further supplied by “I actualised W1” would improve the attribution of the actualisation to G.

- **Defender B**: The argument should distinguish third-personal source-identifying content from practically self-ascriptive cognition that represents actualisation as issuing from that very agent's agency. The relevant condition concerns intentional agency rather than the grammatical form of a proposition. — *This reformulation preserves a possible route to the conclusion, but it revises rather than establishes c008. It leaves open whether the G-thought can itself have the required practical role despite its third-personal form.*

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the argument from divine contrastive responsibility to irreducible de se content depends on a substantive agency condition, not on the merely third-personal grammar or referential precision of divine cognition. It would develop the distinction between source-identifying knowledge and practically self-ascriptive representation, anticipate the rigid-identifier case in which G's intention selects and produces W1, and show either that this case lacks the agency-relative aspect required for robust responsibility or that no such additional aspect is needed. To succeed, the paper would need to state and defend a responsibility-relevant criterion that distinguishes treating an intentional act as an object of knowledge from representing it as one's own doing.

**Revised question:** Does robust contrastive responsibility for actualising one world rather than another require irreducibly de se content in divine cognition, or can a rigid, de re representation of an agent's controlling intention and its act suffice when the agent's identity is not in doubt?

A paper here would argue that the inference from divine contrastive responsibility to irreducible de se content fails, because robust responsibility requires only that the agent's intention be the operative control state for the outcome, and that a rigid, de re representation of that intention satisfies this requirement. The paper would defend the Cappelen-Dever style horn: the functional role of G's intention in selecting and producing W1 is sufficient for source attribution, so no irreducible first-personal mode of presentation is needed. It would make two moves. First, it would concede the referee's strongest point, that forming an intention to actualise W1 involves practical self-ascription, and then distinguish two things that point runs together: practical self-ascription as a feature of the control state (G's intention is G's, and G knows that G is the being whose intention is operative), which is de re and identity-preserving, and de se content as an irreducible mode of presentation that the attribution needs in order to be correct. Second, it would argue that the divine case changes the dialectic in a specific way. In the messy-shopper cases the case for de se content rests on an epistemic gap about who one is, and that gap is absent here, since G has no ignorance of identity and there is only one possible controller. Holding identity knowledge fixed therefore isolates the agency condition itself. The paper would then argue that any remaining case for irreducible de se content must rest on that condition alone, and that the condition is satisfied by the rigid case without residue. The paper would not claim that c008's literal claim is informative. Its target is the 'required for source attribution' clause, which the paper would restate as a claim about what robust responsibility minimally requires.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The paper accepts the referee's premise that intention-formation involves self-ascription, and denies that this yields irreducible de se content. It draws the distinction between de re self-ascription, where the agent's identity with the controlling being is known under a rigid identifier and the intention is recognised as the operative control state, and de se content, where the proposition is individuated by a first-personal mode of presentation that cannot be replaced by a rigid one. The argument is that robust contrastive responsibility needs only the first. In the G-case the functional role of the intention in selecting and producing W1 supplies the agency condition, and the identity knowledge is trivial because G has no candidate for being someone else. The referee's messy-shopper contrast therefore does not carry over: its force depends on an identity gap, which the divine case lacks. The reply concedes that the referee's dilemma holds for any case with an identity gap, and argues that the divine case is not one.
