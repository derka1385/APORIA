## Challenged premise
**W7203485685.c005**: If an agent is robustly responsible for bringing about one outcome rather than another, its cognition must represent the first outcome as its own action.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · anthropic:opus*

Ines, a reservoir controller, faces rising water during a storm. Opening the east spillway will flood farmland; doing nothing will let the dam overtop and flood the town. She studies hydrographs, weighs crop losses against lives, and concludes, in exactly these terms, 'The farmland should flood rather than the town.' She opens the spillway and the farmland floods. The inquiry finds her robustly responsible for the farmland flooding rather than the town: she foresaw it, compared it with the alternative, chose it knowingly, and could have done otherwise. Yet the outcome she represented, the farmland under water, was never represented as her action. It is a hydrological event downstream, and she represented it as one. The only thing she represented as her doing was turning the spillway wheel, and that is a different item from the outcome for which she is held responsible. Her responsibility for the flood rests on representing the flood as a foreseen consequence she was choosing between, not on representing the flood as her action.

## Strongest responses and why they failed
- **Defender A**: The objector is right that the distal outcome need not be represented as the agent's action. Responsibility instead requires representing the selected outcome, under the relevant contrast, as the result of an act the agent performs and intends. — *This concedes the challenged premise as stated. The revision may preserve the wider argument, but it requires a further case that representing an act of actualising as one's own entails irreducible de se content in the divine case.*
- **Defender B**: The relevant source-attribution condition attaches to the act-outcome relation: the agent represents that, by performing a selected intervention, the outcome will result. A merely third-personal identification of an actualised world may still omit the first-personal standpoint of selecting it. — *The reply successfully distinguishes the outcome from the action and retains an agent-relative condition. It does not establish that this condition cannot be met by non-de-se, third-personal divine cognition, especially if God can represent an act as God's own without irreducible first-personal content.*

## Open questions
- Is representing an outcome as issuing from an act one performs sufficient for robust contrastive responsibility, or must the agent also intend the outcome rather than merely foresee it?
- Does source attribution to an act require irreducible de se content, or can it be expressed through a suitably rich third-personal proposition?
- What distinguishes divine actualisation from ordinary cases in which an agent represents a distal consequence as resulting from a chosen intervention?
- Does the revised premise support the original conclusion about divine cognition without adding substantive premises about intention, agency, or self-ascription?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the objection identifies a genuine equivocation between responsibility for an outcome and representing that outcome as an action: robust contrastive responsibility requires an agent-relative representation of the act–outcome relation, not self-representation of the distal outcome as an action. The case would develop the reservoir example and analogous ordinary agency cases, then anticipate the strongest reply that source attribution still requires a first-personal stance toward the selected act. It would need to show either that this stance is available in non-de-se terms, weakening the route to the divine conclusion, or that a principled feature of divine actualisation makes irreducible de se content necessary despite the revised premise.

## Revised after the quality check
*The revision drops the outcome/action equivocation as the main contribution, concedes that the literal premise is false and its repair is cheap, and makes the divine de se conclusion conditional on whether non-indexical identification of basic actualisation as God's own act is available.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** Does robust contrastive responsibility require irreducibly de se representation of the selected outcome, or is agent-relative source attribution, represented in non-de-se terms, enough; and does divine basic action change that answer?

A paper here would argue that the premise as literally stated, that the outcome must be represented as the agent's own action, is false, as the reservoir case shows. It would then argue that the repaired condition, that cognition represent the outcome as resulting from the agent's own act, is agent-relative but can in ordinary agency be met by non-de-se content: the agent describes the act-outcome link in general terms and identifies her own act de re through a perceptually given candidate, the spillway wheel she turns. The paper would then argue that the divine case removes that anchor, since actualising a world is not given among candidate acts and has no proximal/distal gap. It would test the two remaining routes. On the first, God knows de re that God actualises W1, which is non-de-se if God's self-identification is secured without a further self-locating fact. On the second, source attribution requires a first-personal identification of the act as one's own when no other anchor is available, which would make de se content irreducible. The paper would defend a conditional verdict: the divine conclusion holds only if basic actualisation is selected among alternatives and no non-indexical means of identifying it as God's own act is available. It would engage the Perry, Lewis and Castañeda literature on essential indexicals and the Kretzmann and Grim debates on omniscience and indexicals to decide whether that condition is met.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The paper accepts the referee's point that 'resulting from its own action' repairs the premise at no cost, so the outcome/action equivocation is not its main contribution. It also accepts that Ines's own report is de se at the act level. It replies on two fronts. First, it distinguishes self-ascribing the property of being the actualiser from identifying oneself de re under a name or essence; the repaired premise requires only the former if the latter can secure source attribution. Second, it grants that basic action removes the proximal/distal gap, but argues this does not remove the need to identify which act is one's own. The paper therefore tests whether that identification can be non-indexical in the divine case, rather than assuming it.
