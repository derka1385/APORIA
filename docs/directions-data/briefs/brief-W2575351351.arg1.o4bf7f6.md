## Challenged premise
**W2575351351.c014**: Multiple goods may arise from a single instance of nonresistant nonbelief without increasing that evil.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · anthropic:opus*

Mara is a sincere seeker who wants to believe in God but cannot. Consider three goods her nonbelief might produce. First, she shows moral courage by sheltering a refugee without any expectation of divine reward. That needs her nonbelief to last through the crisis, about two months. Second, she comes to deeply understand other doubters. That needs her to feel the full desolation of unanswered searching, which takes a sharper and lonelier phase of doubt, not just mild uncertainty. Third, her brother freely chooses to walk with her through doubt rather than being prompted by evident divine presence. He is slow to choose, so her nonbelief must persist until he does, eighteen months in all. Each good attaches to the same episode of nonbelief, but each requires that episode to be longer or more painful than the previous goods required. Securing all three turns two calm months of doubt into a year and a half of anguish. The goods are not extra fruit from a fixed cost. Each one buys itself by enlarging the evil.

## Open questions
- Can an episode of nonresistant nonbelief be bounded independently of the goods for which God permits it, and if so, what fixes its duration and severity?
- How should the value of a lost relationship with God be measured across episodes that differ in length, intensity, and prospects of later relationship?
- Which goods genuinely arise without requiring additional nonbelief, rather than being already priced into the duration or severity warranted by another good?
- Can the good that warrants a maximally demanding episode plus its cost-free co-realized goods plausibly outweigh that episode’s total relationship-loss?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that a viable many-goods response must distinguish goods that warrant the duration and severity of an episode of nonresistant nonbelief from goods that are genuinely co-realized at no additional relationship-loss. The case would show why aggregating all goods associated with an episode overstates the response when some goods require the episode to become longer or more anguished. It would anticipate the strongest reply: that bounded episodes can contain several cost-free goods and that temporary loss may still be outweighed. It would need to show either that such independently bounded episodes can be specified and valued in a way that supports the defense, or that the dependence of an episode’s cost on its goods makes the required aggregate comparison unavailable.

## Revised after the quality check
*The thesis is narrowed from a claim that aggregation overstates the response to a claim that the aggregate comparison is indeterminate for goods requiring episode extension, while the modal claim of c014 is conceded as surviving the objection.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** Can the aggregate comparison that a many-goods response needs, between the goods of a nonresistant nonbelief episode and its relationship-loss, be made determinate when some of those goods require the episode to be longer or more severe, so that the episode's cost is not fixed independently of the goods it is said to justify?

A paper here would argue that the modal claim that multiple goods may arise from a single episode of nonresistant nonbelief without increasing its evil survives the referee's objection, but that this modal claim does not by itself secure the aggregate comparison the many-goods response needs. The paper would grant that Mara's case shows only that some goods raise the cost, which is compatible with one cost-free co-realization. It would then argue that the net comparison, in which marginal goods are weighed against marginal evil, presupposes that an episode's duration and severity can be fixed independently of the goods it is said to contain. For goods whose realization depends on the episode's extent, such as the brother's slow choice, that presupposition fails. The episode's length is set by the goods it must realize, so the cost assigned to any given good depends on which goods are counted as belonging to the episode. Different selections of goods yield different baselines and different net values, so the aggregate comparison required by the collective-outweighing premise is indeterminate, or question-begging if the goods are used to fix the cost they are weighed against. The paper would develop this as a principled argument about individuation, not a reminder not to double-count, and would engage the greater-goods hiddenness literature on how goods are weighed against relationship-loss. It would conclude that the many-goods response can succeed only for episodes whose duration is specified independently of the goods they support, and that the response's collective claim is therefore restricted to such episodes.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The paper concedes the referee's central point: c014 is a possibility claim, and one cost-free co-occurrence would satisfy it, so Mara's case does not refute it. It rejects the claim that Defender A's net comparison merely restates c014. The reply uses a distinction between the modal possibility of cost-free co-realization and the determinacy of the aggregate comparison. The paper argues that net-value bookkeeping needs a baseline episode whose cost is fixed before its goods are counted. For goods that require extension, such as the brother's delayed choice, the baseline is not fixed independently of the goods, so the marginal-cost assignment depends on which goods are selected. The 'unavailable' branch is therefore argued, not announced: the comparison is indeterminate because the cost function's inputs are the goods it is meant to evaluate.

**Re-assessment** (fresh read, the assessor did not see its earlier critique): **not yet defensible** · overall 3.0/5 · coherence 3/5 · robustness 2/5 · significance 3/5 · specificity 4/5

*A clearly framed but misdiagnosed project: the interdependence of goods and episode cost undermines per-good cost attribution, not the aggregate comparison, so the indeterminacy thesis needs rebuilding before this is a defensible paper.*

- Strongest remaining objection: The indeterminacy argument confuses marginal cost attribution with aggregate comparison. For any candidate set S of goods there is a determinate minimal episode (duration and severity) needed to realize S, and so a determinate total relationship-loss. The many-goods response, and c017 in particular, needs only a holistic comparison of the total value of S against the total loss of that minimal episode, choosing the best package; it never needs to assign a cost to each individual good. As with joint-cost allocation in economics, splitting a shared cost among co-produced goods may be arbitrary, but maximizing total net value is well defined. So the fact that the episode's length is set by the goods it realizes yields neither indeterminacy nor question-begging.
- What it still needs: The paper must show why a package-level comparison (each set of goods against the loss of its minimal sufficient episode) is unavailable or itself indeterminate, for instance through incommensurability, vagueness in 'minimal sufficient episode,' or interdependence that defeats a well-ordering of packages. Otherwise it must drop the indeterminacy thesis and argue directly that the actual escalating packages fail to outweigh. It should also be explicit that its target is c017's aggregate claim rather than c014, which it concedes.
