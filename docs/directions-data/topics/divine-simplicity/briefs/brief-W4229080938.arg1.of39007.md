## Challenged premise
**W4229080938.c001**: Divine simplicity is the claim that God is not a composite of more basic features.

## Objection
*reasoner:minimalist · qwen:Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B*

c001 defines divine simplicity as God not being a composite of 'more basic features.' This smuggles in a hierarchical assumption: that any genuine composition requires parts strictly ontologically prior to the whole. But c011 and c012 describe the actual threat—God depending on power, wisdom, and blessedness as final causes. These are not 'more basic' than God; they are co-equal attributes or modes at the same ontological level. A composite of co-equal features (a whole constituted by aspects none of which is 'more basic' than the others) is excluded by c001's definition yet is precisely the structure c012 fears. The argument needs c001 to rule out that structure, but c001's 'more basic' qualifier makes it incapable of doing so. The premise assumes a part-whole hierarchy the rest of the argument never establishes and that the causal-dependence worry does not require.

## Open questions
- Does the dependence-based revision of c001 risk circularity, given that the argument's unstated major premise already asserts that God is not causally dependent on his distinct properties?
- Can the causal-dependence argument in c009-c012 motivate the stronger traditional claim that God lacks all composition of distinct features, or does it support only the narrower claim that God lacks parts on which he depends?
- Is Ghazālī's non-compositional model of God and attributes compatible with the revised definitions, and does it introduce a form of complexity that the causal argument fails to address?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the causal-dependence argument for divine simplicity cannot rest on the definition of simplicity as the absence of hierarchical composition, because the causal-dependence worry applies equally to co-equal attributes. The case would show that c001 must be revised to exclude either all dependence-grounding composition or all composition of distinct features, and it would trace the consequences of each revision for the argument's validity and metaphysical commitments. The strongest reply to anticipate is that the broader traditional understanding of simplicity already rules out every form of composition, so the objection merely exposes an infelicitous formulation rather than a structural flaw. To succeed, the paper would need to show that the causal-dependence argument by itself cannot motivate the stronger, unrestricted anti-composition claim without relying on independent metaphysical assumptions about part-whole priority, and that the narrower, dependence-based revision either introduces circularity or leaves room for a non-hierarchical but complex conception of the divine that the tradition would reject.
