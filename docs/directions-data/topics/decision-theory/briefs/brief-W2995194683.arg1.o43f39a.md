## Challenged premise
**W2995194683.c001**: A rational person's intentional states are psychologically coherent across time, and rational decisions are made against this coherent backdrop.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · mistral:mistralai/Mistral-Medium-3.5-128B*

Alice is a rational scientist who, at T1, intends to accept a high-paying job if offered by T2. At T2, she is offered the job but, due to new information about unethical practices, decides to reject it. Her intentional states are internally consistent at each time, and her decision is rational given her updated beliefs. Yet her psychological states are not coherent *across time*—her initial intention and later decision conflict—without irrationality.

## Open questions
- Can a principled, non-trivial criterion for rational intention revision be articulated, or does any such criterion make the diachronic coherence constraint too permissive to do the work required by the argument's later premises?
- Does restricting diachronic coherence to an agent's underlying rational dispositions preserve the predictive link between the predictor's forecast and the agent's particular choice, or does it sever the connection that the extended-process model of decision requires?
- If rational agency permits cross-time inconsistency at the level of particular intentions, what independent reason is there to think that an extended, coherent decision process determines the one-boxing choice rather than a sequence of locally rational updates?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that diachronic coherence-based defenses of one-boxing in Newcomb's Problem cannot rely on an unrestricted premise that all intentional states cohere across time, because rational agents routinely revise particular intentions in light of new evidence without irrationality. The case would develop the Alice-style counterexample to show that the original premise overstates the stability of rational psychology, then examine whether a narrowed stability condition—framed in terms of underlying rational dispositions or evidence-responsive revision norms—can both survive the counterexample and still underwrite the argument's claim that the decision is an extended process bound to the predictor's forecast. The strongest reply to anticipate is that the argument never needed strict persistence of every intention, but only a stable rational backdrop that predictors can exploit. The paper would need to show that such a narrowed condition is non-trivial, principled, and robust enough to preserve the link between coherence, the extended-process model of decision, and the predictor's nomic success without collapsing into a vacuous or causally reductive reading of rational agency.

- Strongest remaining objection: The paper's central move says reconsideration norms are 'silent' when no new evidence arrives, but standard accounts of those norms (Bratman's non-reconsideration, Holton's resolution) are not silent then. Absent new evidence, they require the agent to keep the prior intention or plan. So the no-new-evidence stipulation does not open a gap between disposition and token choice. A further problem is the claim that Newcomb's Problem stipulates no relevant new evidence. The chooser does learn something at choice time: the box setup, and with it the salience of the dominance reasoning. That is the standard two-boxer's ground for reconsideration, and the paper does not address it.
