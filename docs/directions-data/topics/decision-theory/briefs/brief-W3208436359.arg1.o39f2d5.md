## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · kimi:moonshotai/Kimi-K2.6*

A firefighter chooses between two rescue plans. Plan A saves five people for certain; the outcome is complete in ten minutes, with a determinate finite value. Plan B promises to save one additional person every minute thereafter forever, labeled with surreal utility ω. The commander asks the crew to maximize expected lives saved, but Plan B never reaches a terminal state: after any finite time the tally is finite, and there is no moment when the infinite total is realized. Assigning a transfinite utility requires positing a completed limit the crew cannot operationalize—payoffs, probabilities, and outcomes all presuppose a determinate final state. The finite problem is complete and actionable; the transfinite analogue lacks the well-formed terminal outcome necessary for a coherent decision matrix. Because the infinite rescue has no determinate terminus, its utility assignment is parasitic on a non-existent final world-state.

## Open questions
- Does the action-guiding character of expected utility theory require that every utility correspond to a determinate terminal world-state, or can mathematically specified limits function as adequate relata?
- Is the restriction of transfinite decision theory to finite state spaces with determinate outcomes a principled boundary or an ad hoc retreat from the broader ambition of handling infinite-horizon processes?
- If finite expected utility already evaluates acts by expectations over unrealized states, what additional constraints must be met for transfinite analogues to preserve the same normative structure?
- Can a surreal-valued decision problem be well-formed when its utilities are attached to sequences that never yield a terminal state, and if so, what ontology of outcomes would ground those assignments?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the well-formedness of transfinite decision problems requires a determinate terminal outcome for every utility assignment, and that using surreal numbers to label infinite-horizon processes conflates formal definability with genuine decision-theoretic well-formedness. The case would turn on the firefighter counterexample: because Plan B never yields a terminal world-state, the surreal value ω lacks a relatum in the state space, producing a formally calculable expectation that is not a well-formed decision matrix. The strongest reply to anticipate is that the target argument restricts itself to finite state spaces with determinate terminal outcomes, so the counterexample strikes only a broader formulation that the revised premise already abandons. To succeed, the paper would need to show either that this restriction is ad hoc and surrenders the ambition of a general transfinite decision theory, or that even within finite state spaces the shift from finite to surreal utilities introduces a discontinuity in outcome-determinacy that the surreal apparatus cannot bridge.

*The thesis is narrowed from a terminal-outcome requirement to a determinate-outcome condition, which the complete-history and lexical cases both satisfy, and the potentialist premise is made an explicit conditional rather than a load-bearing claim.* The thesis was narrowed.

**Revised question:** Is a transfinite decision problem well-formed when each utility is assigned to a determinate outcome, whether that outcome is a complete history of a non-terminating process or a lexically specified good, and does the surreal framework respect that determinacy?

A paper here would argue, in narrower form, that a transfinite decision problem is well-formed whenever every act maps every state to a uniquely determined outcome in the outcome space, and that this determinacy condition does not require a temporally terminal world-state. Outcomes may be complete histories of non-terminating processes, which are fully determinate even though they are never completed at any moment, or lexically specified goods to which a surreal utility is assigned directly. On this view the firefighter case is well-formed: Plan B's outcome is the complete trajectory fixed by the act, so its utility of ω attaches to a determinate relatum and the 'no terminus' objection fails. The terminal-state requirement is dropped because it would also invalidate finite-valued infinite-horizon theories such as discounted reward streams and infinite-horizon MDPs, which are accepted as well-formed. The paper then argues that surreal numbers can serve as the utility scale for such determinate outcomes, using the conservative-extension result for finitist expected utility theory to show that the transfinite assignments add no inconsistency to the finite theory. The paper concedes that a potentialist who denies that a complete infinite history is an actual outcome can reject the complete-history reading. The claim is therefore conditional: it holds within an actualist treatment of outcomes, and the potentialist question about actual infinities is treated as a separate metaphysical issue that the decision-theoretic thesis does not need to settle. The original general ambition is restricted to the class of transfinite problems with determinate outcomes, and the aggregative-limit source of infinite value is treated as one case within that class, not the whole of it.

**How the paper answers the main objection:** The referee's objection is decisive against the terminal-state requirement, so the paper drops that requirement rather than defending it. It replies with a distinction between a terminal state and a determinate outcome. Expected utility theory requires only that each act assign a unique outcome to each state, and a complete infinite history satisfies this without being completed at any time. This answers the referee's point that the requirement is imported and would condemn finite-valued infinite-horizon theory. For lexical superiority, the paper argues that the same determinacy condition is met by directly specified goods, so the thesis covers non-aggregative sources as well. The paper does not claim the potentialist premise is false; it limits its claim to actualist outcome spaces and states this explicitly.

*There is a clear thesis here, but as planned it would be stopped by the first referee who asks why the infinite history is worth ω rather than any other infinite surreal, so it is not yet a defensible paper.*

- Strongest remaining objection: Showing that the outcome is determinate does not show that its utility is determinate. Grant that Plan B's complete history is a well-defined relatum. It still has no canonical surreal value. The surreals are not Cauchy-complete and lack a limit operation, so the sum 1+1+1+... does not converge to ω rather than ω−1, ω/2 or √ω. The attachment of ω to the history is therefore a stipulation rather than a determination. If every candidate assignment is equally licensed, then the comparative verdicts that make a decision problem action-guiding are hostage to the choice. That is exactly the respect in which the problem is less well-formed than its finite analogue. The infinite-horizon MDP analogy does not help. Those models are well-formed because discounting secures convergence to a unique real value, not merely because outcomes are determinate trajectories. The conservative-extension result establishes only consistency with finite EU theory, not the non-arbitrariness of the transfinite assignments.
- What it still needs: The paper must add a principled account of how a determinate infinite outcome fixes a unique surreal utility, or at least a unique utility ordering under the relevant operations, for example via a specified summation or valuation rule with independent motivation. It must also show that decision verdicts are invariant across the admissible choices. Separately, it must replace the MDP analogy with an argument that does not depend on convergence.
