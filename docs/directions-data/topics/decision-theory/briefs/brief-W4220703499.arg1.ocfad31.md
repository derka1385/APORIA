## Challenged premise
**W4220703499.c019**: At T1 S cannot choose between A and B because he cannot be choosing before he is choosing.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · anthropic:sonnet*

Consider Maya, a chess player at T1 who is weighing two moves, A (Nf3) and B (d4). She deliberates for ten seconds, then plays A. Her premise is that she is choosing now, while the options are open. Suppose a rival says: 'At T1 you cannot choose between A and B, since choosing is something you can only be doing after you have chosen.' Test this against Maya's case. Her deliberation is itself the process of choosing: weighing reasons, rejecting B, settling on A. Nothing in that process requires the choice to be complete before it begins. If choosing could occur only after it had finished, no one could ever deliberate toward a decision, and Maya's ten seconds would be idle. The inference treats 'is choosing' as a state that must already hold, when for a free agent it is the activity that makes the choice occur. The claim that one cannot be choosing before one is choosing is true only if 'choosing' names a completed event, which begs the question about whether T1 is open.

## Open questions
- If deliberation is itself a process of choosing, can any premise like c019 distinguish between an agent's being open to alternatives and the choice's being undetermined without begging the question against libertarianism?
- Does the Grounding Principle entail that God's eternal knowledge of a choice is logically posterior to the choice, and if so, can that knowledge still be prior to every temporal event in the way eternalism requires?
- Can an eternalist theory of time block the inference from 'God eternally knows that S will choose A' to 'S's outcome is fixed during deliberation' without surrendering the claim that divine knowledge is genuinely foreknowledge?
- Is the determinative act of choosing a punctiliar event, or does the extended process of deliberation itself satisfy the Anselmian thesis's requirement that the agent choose with aseity?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that reconciling divine foreknowledge with libertarian freedom on an eternalist theory of time requires recognizing deliberation as constitutive of choosing, which undermines the premise that an agent cannot be choosing before that agent is choosing. The case would draw on the extended-process model of agency to show that treating choosing as an activity unfolding over time makes the original c019 either false or question-begging, and would then press the dilemma that either God's eternal perception fixes the outcome during deliberation, violating the open-options condition, or it does not, rendering the prohibition on prior choosing redundant. The strongest reply to anticipate is that God's knowledge is grounded in the settled choice alone, not in the deliberative process, so the paper would need to show that this grounding relation still makes the eternal fact of God's knowledge prior to the choice in a way that collapses the distinction between logical posteriority and temporal fixity, thereby demonstrating that the eternalist reconciliation fails once deliberation is taken seriously.

The paper therefore concedes that c019 is true and drops the claim that it bans deliberation. The live question shifts to where settledness falls. It would then press the question whether the open-options condition at c006 requires that the choice be unsettled at every time before its occurrence, and whether the atemporalist can accept that settledness is a feature of the choice's own temporal profile, not of God's knowledge, without assuming that eternity is temporally prior to events. **How the paper answers the main objection:** The referee is right that the Maya case attacks a strawman, since c019 does not deny deliberation. The paper concedes this and treats c019 as an instance of the c010 internal necessity, so the premise stands. The substantive challenge is then the one Hasker presses: whether the Grounding Principle at c007 yields temporal fixity at some time during deliberation. The paper answers with the distinction between logical posteriority and temporal fixity. Eternal knowledge is logically posterior to the actual choice because the truth of the knowledge depends on that choice, but the eternal knowledge is located at no time and so fixes nothing at any time. Fixity, if it arises, is a feature of the choice's own temporal profile, specifying when the option is no longer open. The paper argues that the atemporalist can accept settledness as a temporal property of the choice without granting that eternity is temporally prior to events. The paper does not assume eternal priority.
